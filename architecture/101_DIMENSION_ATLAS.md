# The 101-Dimension Atlas
**Status:** foundational working ontology  
**Version:** 0.1.0  
**Purpose:** provide multiple independent-but-interacting lenses for examining people, events, systems, environments, ideas, and change.

## The premise

The 101 dimensions are not 101 compartments of reality. They are 101 distinguishable questions that can be asked of the same reality. A dimension is a lens, not a wall. Dimensions overlap, nest, interact, emerge at different scales, and may be useful only in particular contexts.

The number 101 establishes the first shared atlas. It is not a claim that reality has exactly 101 dimensions, nor a limit on future discovery. New dimensions may be added, split, merged, renamed, or retired with their history preserved.

No single dimension is the master axis. Time is represented richly, but does not govern every relationship. Spatial, causal, experiential, informational, symbolic, and relational connections may cross chronology. Historical records retain sequence and provenance without assuming that sequence exhausts meaning.

## The 101 dimensions

### I. Existence and materiality

1. **Existence** — whether something is present, absent, possible, actual, or indeterminate.
2. **Identity** — what makes an entity recognizable across difference and change.
3. **Composition** — what parts, substances, agents, or elements constitute it.
4. **Structure** — how components are organized and constrained.
5. **Substrate** — the medium or material basis through which something exists or operates.
6. **Boundary** — what is treated as inside, outside, interface, or threshold.
7. **Scale** — the level of magnitude or organization being examined.
8. **State** — the configuration or condition at a particular observation.
9. **Potentiality** — capacities or possibilities not currently expressed.
10. **Multiplicity** — the ways something may be plural, layered, distributed, or simultaneously more than one thing.

### II. Space and configuration

11. **Position** — location relative to a reference frame.
12. **Distance** — separation or proximity, physical or abstract.
13. **Direction** — orientation, vector, or movement tendency.
14. **Topology** — connectivity and neighborhood independent of exact metric distance.
15. **Geometry** — shape, angle, curvature, proportion, and spatial relation.
16. **Containment** — what holds, surrounds, embeds, or hosts something.
17. **Accessibility** — whether a path, resource, place, or interface can be reached or used.
18. **Orientation** — the frame from which an entity or observer is situated.
19. **Density** — concentration of entities, events, signals, or relations in a region.
20. **Scale-transition** — what changes or remains invariant when moving between micro, meso, macro, and global views.

### III. Temporality and change

21. **Sequence** — ordering of events or states in a chosen record.
22. **Duration** — how long a state, event, or process persists.
23. **Recurrence** — repetition, periodicity, rhythm, or return.
24. **Simultaneity** — events or processes co-occurring within a chosen frame.
25. **Latency** — delay between an input, event, decision, or signal and its detectable response.
26. **Anticipation** — orientation toward possible futures and expected events.
27. **Retrospection** — reinterpretation of earlier events from a later standpoint.
28. **Transition** — passage between states, phases, regimes, or identities.
29. **Historical situatedness** — what was knowable, available, or understood from a particular context without later knowledge being projected backward.
30. **Temporal depth** — the range of past, present, and possible future horizons relevant to a pattern.

### IV. Energy, dynamics, and causation

31. **Energy** — capacity to produce change or perform work, broadly construed within the relevant domain.
32. **Flow** — movement of matter, energy, information, attention, or resources.
33. **Exchange** — transfer between entities or systems.
34. **Feedback** — outputs that return to influence subsequent system behavior.
35. **Coupling** — degree and manner in which components influence one another.
36. **Causation** — mechanisms or interventions that produce differences, distinguished from correlation.
37. **Constraint** — conditions that limit possible states or trajectories.
38. **Threshold** — boundary at which behavior, state, or response changes qualitatively.
39. **Nonlinearity** — disproportionate, context-dependent, or non-additive response.
40. **Emergence** — system-level patterns not reducible to a simple sum of component properties.

### V. Life, body, and ecology

41. **Physiology** — functional processes within a living organism.
42. **Morphology** — form, shape, and structural organization of living systems.
43. **Metabolism** — transformation and use of matter and energy.
44. **Signaling** — communication within and between biological components.
45. **Regulation** — mechanisms that maintain, adjust, or coordinate biological states.
46. **Adaptation** — change that improves fit or function under particular conditions, over relevant timescales.
47. **Development** — growth, differentiation, maturation, and organized change.
48. **Degeneration** — loss or breakdown of structure or function.
49. **Regeneration** — repair, restoration, or replacement of damaged or lost structure and function.
50. **Ecological interdependence** — reciprocal dependencies among organisms and their living and nonliving environments.

### VI. Sensation, cognition, and learning

51. **Sensation** — registration of internal or external stimuli.
52. **Perception** — organization and interpretation of sensory input.
53. **Interoception** — sensing internal bodily conditions.
54. **Proprioception** — sensing body position, movement, and force.
55. **Attention** — selective allocation of processing or awareness.
56. **Memory** — encoding, retention, reconstruction, and retrieval.
57. **Inference** — deriving conclusions beyond directly observed information.
58. **Learning** — relatively durable change in capability or model through experience.
59. **Prediction** — estimation of likely states or events.
60. **Metacognition** — monitoring and regulation of one's own cognition and uncertainty.

### VII. Emotion, relation, and experience

61. **Affect** — felt valence, intensity, and activation.
62. **Emotion** — organized affective responses involving appraisal, physiology, action tendency, and meaning.
63. **Motivation** — forces that initiate, direct, sustain, or inhibit action.
64. **Salience** — what stands out as significant to an observer or system.
65. **Meaning** — significance assigned or discovered within a context.
66. **Trust** — willingness to rely under uncertainty and vulnerability.
67. **Attachment** — enduring bonds and patterns of proximity, care, or reliance.
68. **Reciprocity** — mutual influence, exchange, or response.
69. **Conflict** — incompatibility among needs, goals, interpretations, values, or system states.
70. **Belonging** — experienced or recognized inclusion within a group, place, identity, or relation.

### VIII. Social, cultural, and institutional

71. **Agency** — capacity to initiate, choose, influence, or refuse.
72. **Power** — ability to shape options, outcomes, resources, or interpretations.
73. **Role** — position carrying expectations, permissions, and responsibilities.
74. **Normativity** — explicit and implicit standards governing what is expected or acceptable.
75. **Culture** — shared and contested practices, meanings, symbols, and learned patterns.
76. **Language** — systems for expression, reference, coordination, and thought.
77. **Institution** — durable rules, organizations, procedures, and authority structures.
78. **Access** — practical ability to obtain resources, services, participation, or opportunity.
79. **Distribution** — allocation of resources, risks, burdens, benefits, and visibility.
80. **Collective behavior** — patterns that arise through group interaction and coordination.

### IX. Information, evidence, and knowledge

81. **Information** — distinctions or signals that reduce uncertainty for a receiver or process.
82. **Representation** — the form through which something is encoded, modeled, or depicted.
83. **Resolution** — granularity and detail available in an observation or model.
84. **Noise** — variation or interference that obscures a signal relative to a task.
85. **Missingness** — what is absent from the record and the process producing that absence.
86. **Provenance** — where a record came from and how it was transformed.
87. **Evidence** — information bearing on the support or challenge of a claim.
88. **Uncertainty** — limits on what can be known, measured, inferred, or predicted.
89. **Contradiction** — incompatibility among records, claims, models, or observations.
90. **Interpretation** — a context-dependent account of what information may mean.

### X. Action, ethics, creativity, and value

91. **Action** — what an agent or system does, including omission and inhibition.
92. **Intention** — the aim or purpose toward which action is directed.
93. **Choice** — selection among perceived alternatives under constraints.
94. **Consequence** — effects that follow, including unintended and delayed effects.
95. **Responsibility** — attribution of duties, answerability, and obligations.
96. **Ethics** — evaluation of harm, care, justice, rights, and what ought to be done.
97. **Aesthetics** — form, sensory organization, expressive quality, and felt coherence.
98. **Symbolism** — meanings carried through images, metaphors, signs, and recurring forms.
99. **Narrative** — organization of events and meanings into an intelligible account.
100. **Play** — exploratory activity that permits variation, discovery, and non-instrumental engagement.
101. **Value** — what is considered important, desirable, costly, beneficial, or worth preserving.

## How dimensions work together

A dimension is not a data field that must always be filled. It is a question that may become relevant. A trace can engage one dimension or dozens. The system should not force exhaustive tagging.

Relationships between dimensions must be typed rather than represented by an unexplained line. Useful relationship types include:

- **influences** — a plausible directional effect; not automatically causal proof;
- **constrains** — limits the range of another dimension;
- **enables** — makes a state or action possible;
- **amplifies / dampens** — increases or decreases magnitude;
- **couples** — changes in one are linked to changes in another;
- **transforms** — changes the form or interpretation of another;
- **contradicts** — incompatible observations or interpretations;
- **depends on** — requires another condition or component;
- **co-occurs with** — appears together without a causal claim;
- **is observed through** — connects a phenomenon to a measurement or perspective;
- **is represented by** — connects a phenomenon to a model, symbol, or record;
- **emerges from** — proposes a system-level pattern arising from interactions;
- **is analogous to** — marks a structural resemblance without claiming shared mechanism;
- **is nested within** — relates scales or containing systems;
- **is invariant across** — identifies a property that persists under a transformation.

Every relationship should carry its own provenance, perspective, context, confidence, and revision history. A connection may be useful, disputed, speculative, or disproven.

## The atlas is not a flat coordinate system

The 101 dimensions do not imply that every phenomenon can be assigned a single numeric coordinate. Some dimensions are measurable; others are categorical, relational, experiential, symbolic, or only partially observable. Some are observer-dependent. Some exist at different scales. Some become meaningful only in relation to others.

A future implementation should support:
- many-to-many relationships and hyperedges (one event involving several dimensions at once);
- multiple observers and conflicting perspectives;
- different scales and nested systems;
- continuous, categorical, ordinal, symbolic, and unknown values;
- explicit non-applicability and missing data;
- changing definitions with preserved version history;
- alternate models that disagree without being prematurely merged;
- cross-temporal and atemporal relations alongside dated records;
- user-defined dimensions and local vocabularies;
- uncertainty and evidence attached to each claim and relationship.

## Temporal precision without temporal supremacy

The atlas preserves dates and sequence when they matter for provenance, safety, reproducibility, or historical reconstruction. It does not assume that chronology is the only ordering principle or that later always means more meaningful.

A record may connect to another through resemblance, recurrence, resonance, shared structure, causal dependence, counterfactual relation, or retrospective recognition. Those relations need not be reducible to “A happened before B.” Conversely, a timeless or nonlinear interpretation does not erase the practical importance of when evidence was collected or a decision was made.

## Rules against dimensional overreach

1. Do not treat a lens as the thing itself.
2. Do not assume every dimension applies to every subject.
3. Do not collapse distinct dimensions merely because they correlate.
4. Do not infer causation from co-occurrence, analogy, or narrative fit.
5. Do not force subjective experience into false numerical precision.
6. Do not privilege measurable dimensions over meaningful but less measurable ones.
7. Do not privilege symbolic resonance over evidence.
8. Do not assume one observer's perspective is complete.
9. Do not let a later interpretation overwrite what was understood earlier.
10. Do not treat this first 101 as exhaustive or immutable.

## The operating question

For any phenomenon, ask:

**Which dimensions make this visible, which dimensions are missing, how do they interact, at what scale, from whose perspective, and what would change our interpretation?**

The atlas is successful when it reveals relationships and blind spots without pretending to contain the whole of reality.
