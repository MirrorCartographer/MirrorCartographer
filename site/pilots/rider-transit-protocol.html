Kannapolis · Rider Transit · Draft 1.0

Trip information, without the guesswork.

A small usability study to test whether people can locate, interpret, and verify the information needed to plan an essential transit trip.

PROPOSED PILOT · NO AGENCY ENDORSEMENT
No partner agreement, participant recruitment, or field research is represented as complete. This protocol is a proposal. Do not treat the prototype as an official transit service.

1. The question

Can riders and prospective riders reliably find the route, stop, direction, scheduled time, fare, accessibility resource, and service alert they need using existing official information? The prevalence of confusion has not been established. The first phase measures a baseline rather than assuming a problem.

2. The intervention

A mobile-first, non-authoritative information navigator that points back to official Rider Transit and city sources, labels the source and last-check time, distinguishes schedules from live tracking, and offers print-friendly instructions. It must not invent arrivals, guarantee transfers, scrape restricted feeds, impersonate the operator, or make eligibility or service decisions.

3. Discovery before recruitment

  1. Request a meeting with Rider Transit and relevant city/county contacts.
  2. Ask whether public rider feedback, call-center themes, accessibility requests, and rider education materials can be shared lawfully.
  3. Agree a consent, compensation, privacy, accessibility, and safeguarding process before inviting participants.
  4. Only then conduct 8–12 listening sessions and a task-based usability pilot with a target of 12–20 adult volunteers.
  5. If no measurable information-navigation problem appears, publish the null result and stop or redesign.

4. Study design

Participants complete comparable synthetic trip-planning tasks first with current official materials and then with the prototype; task order should be counterbalanced where practical. Tasks cover route/stop discovery, direction, timetable interpretation, fare/pass information, accessibility resources, and service alerts. This is a usability pilot, not a representative population survey.

5. Measures

  • Correct task completion rate.
  • Critical planning error rate: wrong route, direction, stop, or missed service constraint.
  • Time to correct answer and source verification rate.
  • Confidence calibration against actual correctness.
  • Keyboard and screen-reader task completion; understanding of scheduled versus live data.

6. Predeclared decision thresholds

Consider a larger, partner-reviewed pilot only if critical planning errors decrease by at least 30%, overall task completion improves by at least 20 percentage points or reaches 90%, median completion time improves by at least 20% without a correctness decline, no critical accessibility regression appears, all instructions trace to current official sources, and no material privacy or safety incident occurs. These are pilot thresholds, not statistical significance claims.

7. Stop conditions

Pause if official sources conflict or become stale, an instruction could cause a missed essential service, accessibility information is misleading, consent is inadequate, privacy/security is compromised, or the agency requests suspension. Roll back to direct official links.

8. Privacy and authority

No names, precise home addresses, health information, or trip histories are collected by default. The transit operator retains authority over routes, fares, schedules, service changes, and accessibility determinations. Obtain written permission before using agency marks, nonpublic data, or claiming partnership.

9. Public outputs

Publish the task protocol, baseline report, versioned prototype, source manifest, privacy/accessibility statement, predeclared metrics, correction log, and a clear decision to continue, modify, pause, reverse, or reject. Include null and adverse findings.
