# Ownership, Attribution, and Sustainable Funding Strategy
Version: 1.0.0-draft
Status: operating strategy; obtain qualified legal and tax advice before entity, license, or filing decisions.

## 1. Separate the assets
Maintain an asset register with: asset ID, title, creator/contributors, date, repository/path, commit SHA, source materials, third-party dependencies, AI assistance disclosure where relevant, license, current owner/rights holder, and restrictions.
Asset classes:
- Human-authored text, specifications, diagrams, original art, music, and audiovisual work.
- Software source code and tests.
- Model architecture, tokenizer, training/evaluation code, and checkpoints.
- Datasets, annotations, and provenance manifests.
- Names, logos, domains, and product identities.
- Research methods, know-how, and confidential operational material.
- User contributions and community submissions.

## 2. Establish chain of title before monetization
1. Identify the legal person or entity that currently owns each asset; do not infer ownership solely from a GitHub username or repository name.
2. Record all human contributors and obtain written contributor agreements for commissioned or collaborative work.
3. Audit third-party libraries, fonts, images, music, datasets, and model weights for license compatibility.
4. Preserve dated source files, commits, design decisions, drafts, and human creative contributions.
5. Keep private records and secrets out of public repositories.
6. Do not claim exclusive copyright over purely AI-generated expression. Document human-authored selection, arrangement, editing, and expressive contributions.
7. Do not use a prompt transcript as the sole proof of authorship or invention.

## 3. Attribution policy
Default public credit:
"Created and directed by Charity Sturgell. Research, design, implementation, and contributions are identified in the project provenance and contributor records."
Use only where accurate for the specific artifact. Add named collaborators and AI tool assistance where material. Never imply a model is a human co-author or a conscious independent collaborator.
Each release includes: version, commit, date, contributors, source materials, license, and known limitations.

## 4. Licensing architecture
Do not apply one blanket license to all repositories until asset ownership and dependencies are audited.
- Software: select a recognized open-source license only after deciding whether reuse, modification, redistribution, and patent grants are intended.
- Research documents: select a documentation license separately from code.
- Art/music/film: keep rights reserved or use an explicit Creative Commons license per asset.
- Data: publish only when rights, consent, privacy, and re-identification risk have been reviewed.
- Model weights: publish only with training-data provenance, evaluation report, acceptable-use terms, and a decision about commercial redistribution.
Record the license at file or directory level where mixed rights exist.

## 5. Brand and identity
Treat Mirror Cartographer, LYR, The Observatory, and related names as candidate marks, not automatically protected marks. Search for confusingly similar existing marks before investing in registration. Trademark protects source-identifying marks, not the underlying method or every use of a word. Do not spend on filings before deciding which name will identify a real product/service and who owns it.

## 6. Funding sequence — do not base operations on speculative income
Stage A — zero/low-cost foundation:
- Use existing repositories, free CI, local development, and small reproducible tests.
- Maintain a cash-cost ledger before adding hosted inference, GPUs, storage, or paid APIs.
- No paid compute until a benchmark establishes need and an affordable ceiling is approved.

Stage B — paid work with immediate deliverables:
- Offer fixed-scope research/provenance audits, workflow documentation, accessibility and information-navigation evaluations, or custom prototype work.
- Sell a defined service, not a promise of future societal transformation.
- Use written scope, price, acceptance criteria, payment schedule, data-processing terms, and ownership terms.

Stage C — recurring earned revenue:
- Maintenance/support contracts, hosted deployments, training, and institutional licenses only after repeatable demand and support costs are measured.
- Keep public-interest core functions available under a clearly stated access model where financially feasible.
- Never monetize private user data or use coercive lock-in.

Stage D — external capital:
- Apply for grants or public contracts only when eligibility, sponsor fit, deliverables, reporting burden, matching requirements, and indirect costs are verified.
- Donations or sponsorships are not operating revenue until received.
- Investment is optional and requires a control, dilution, and mission-protection analysis.

## 7. Entity decision
Do not form an LLC, nonprofit, or hybrid merely to look established. First establish ownership, revenue model, liability profile, tax implications, public-benefit commitments, and governance needs with a North Carolina attorney and CPA.
North Carolina Secretary of State currently lists $125 for LLC Articles of Organization and $200 annual report fee (plus applicable electronic transaction fees); confirm the live fee schedule before filing. A nonprofit structure is not a shortcut to personal income and imposes separate governance and tax constraints.

## 8. Financial controls
- Separate personal and project funds once a lawful entity and bank account exist.
- Require approval for recurring subscriptions and compute spend.
- Record actual revenue, direct costs, taxes, refunds, support burden, and runway.
- Never use grant applications or forecast licensing as proof of funds.
- Do not incur debt or guarantee project expenses against uncontracted future income.

## 9. Quarterly review
Review asset chain of title, contributor records, licenses, brand use, data rights, revenue concentration, compute costs, contractual obligations, and mission drift. Publish a short accountability statement for public-facing products.
