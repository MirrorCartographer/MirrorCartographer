# A More Connected Society
## A research argument for shared knowledge, human capability, and technology that works across different ways of living

**Mirror Cartographer — public-interest research direction**  
**Originating project: Charity Sturgell**  
**Status:** Research thesis and program proposal, not a validated social-science finding or a claim of sole originality.  
**Version:** 1.0 · 30 October 2026

---

## Executive thesis

The next major advance in technology should not be judged only by the power, speed, intelligence, or convenience of individual tools. It should also be judged by whether people, institutions, technologies, economies, and ecological systems can coordinate more effectively while preserving human agency and local knowledge.

The United States has substantial scientific capacity, productive knowledge, communications infrastructure, creative ability, and community institutions. Yet these resources are distributed across systems with different incentives, vocabularies, data structures, time horizons, and measures of success. The resulting problem is not simply that information is missing. It is that useful information, capability, and decision-making are difficult to connect across boundaries.

Mirror Cartographer proposes to investigate a public-interest architecture for improving those connections.

The proposal is not to put everyone on one platform, require everyone to use AI, digitize every activity, or centralize social life. It is to make shared progress possible through multiple interfaces—including digital, offline, local, institutional, and in-person routes—so people can contribute and benefit without being forced into one mode of participation.

**The measure of progress should be whether people's lives become healthier, more capable, more independent, more secure, more meaningful, and more full of opportunity—and whether those gains are broadly and fairly distributed.**

## 1. The problem is fragmentation between systems

A person experiences society as a connected whole. Institutions and technologies often encounter that person through separate systems:

- Healthcare records may describe diagnoses and encounters but not fully capture lived function, continuity, or the conditions that shape recovery.
- Agricultural research may produce useful findings, while the practical ability to access, interpret, afford, and apply them varies by place and operation.
- Social platforms make communication and discovery easy but are generally optimized around platform-specific engagement and business models, not necessarily the long-term quality of public understanding.
- Research institutions develop specialized knowledge, but findings do not automatically become accessible tools, local capacity, or improved everyday decisions.
- Public services may be distributed across agencies, eligibility rules, portals, contractors, and offices that require people to coordinate the system themselves.
- Technology products may work well in ideal connectivity and usage conditions but fail to fit intermittent connectivity, physical work, disability, limited time, low trust, or a deliberate preference for non-digital interaction.

These are examples of coordination problems, not proof that every institution or platform fails in the same way. Each domain has legitimate constraints and valuable existing practices. The research question is whether their interfaces can be improved.

### The central distinction

A society can contain excellent individual components and still produce poor combined outcomes.

A sensor can be accurate while its output is unusable to the person who needs it. A research finding can be robust while remaining inaccessible to the community that could apply it. A public benefit can exist while the administrative pathway to obtain it is confusing or burdensome. A social platform can connect millions of people while leaving local knowledge difficult to find or preserve.

This is a systems-design problem: the performance of the whole depends not only on the quality of its parts, but also on the relationships among them.

## 2. Different communities are not different species of person

The shorthand categories—social-media users, technology users, farmers, and people who do not use phones—are useful only as entry points. They are not fixed or mutually exclusive populations.

A farmer may use GPS-guided equipment, cellular data, satellite services, online marketplaces, and precision agriculture while avoiding social media. A creator may be highly digitally fluent but disconnected from food production, infrastructure, or scientific research. A person who does not own a smartphone may have deep practical expertise and strong community networks. An older adult may use some digital services and reject others. A rural resident may be highly connected in one setting and underserved in another.

The relevant dimensions are not simply online/offline. They include:
- access, affordability, reliability, and device availability;
- digital skills, accessibility, language, and confidence;
- relevance of a tool to a person's actual work and goals;
- trust, privacy, ownership, and control;
- local expertise and practical constraints;
- ability to participate without continuous connectivity;
- the right to decline a technology without losing access to essential services.

The design objective is not universal adoption. It is **equitable usefulness across different modes of participation**.

## 3. What the available evidence says—and does not say

The evidence shows that digital participation is widespread but uneven, and that agricultural technology use is more varied than stereotypes suggest.

### U.S. household connectivity

Pew Research Center's 2025 survey reports that 78% of U.S. adults subscribe to home broadband. The reported rate is 54% among adults in households earning under $30,000, compared with 94% among those in households earning $100,000 or more. The survey reports 71% home broadband subscription among rural adults. These are survey estimates of household subscription, not a measure of whether a person can successfully use every service or whether service is affordable, reliable, accessible, and appropriate.

Source: [Pew Research Center, Internet use, smartphone ownership and digital divides in the U.S. (January 8, 2026)](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/01/08/internet-use-smartphone-ownership-digital-divides-in-u-s/).

### Farm technology

USDA's 2025 Farm Computer Usage and Ownership report states that 85% of farms reported internet access; 55% used a broadband connection; 74% had access through a cellular data plan; and 82% had a smartphone. It also reports that 50% used the internet to purchase agricultural inputs and 29% used it to market agricultural activities.

These figures do not support a simplistic picture of farmers as technology outsiders. They show adoption alongside differences in connection type, business use, and the practical fit of technologies. Farm internet access, broadband use, smartphone use, and precision-agriculture adoption are different measures and should not be conflated.

Sources:
- [USDA NASS, Technology Use (Farm Computer Usage and Ownership), August 2025](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Todays_Reports/reports/fmpc0825.pdf).
- [USDA National Agricultural Library publication record](https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/publication/technology-use-farm-computer-usage-and-ownership).

### Existing bridges already work

The United States does not begin from zero. The Cooperative Extension System connects federal support, land-grant universities, and local extension agents to share research-based knowledge with communities. USDA describes Extension as a way to bring university research and information to people in every county.

This is a critical precedent: knowledge transfer works through relationships and local institutions, not merely through publishing information online. MC should study, support, and interoperate with such institutions rather than present itself as their replacement.

Source: [USDA NIFA, Cooperative Extension System](https://www.nifa.usda.gov/about-nifa/how-we-work/extension/cooperative-extension-system).

### Evidence boundary

These sources establish that connectivity and technology use are widespread but unequal, and that public knowledge-translation institutions already exist. They do **not** establish that a new shared platform would improve national outcomes. That is a hypothesis to test.

## 4. The argument: optimize the relationships, not only the components

The proposal rests on five linked claims.

### Claim 1 — Important knowledge is distributed

Useful knowledge exists in scientific literature, professional practice, local observation, lived experience, traditional and community knowledge, operational records, and creative work. No single institution or interface contains all of it.

This does not mean every claim has equal evidentiary status. A farmer's observation, a controlled trial, a sensor reading, and a personal interpretation are different kinds of evidence. A useful system preserves those distinctions while allowing them to inform questions together.

### Claim 2 — Knowledge does not create public value automatically

For knowledge to improve outcomes, people must be able to find it, understand its relevance, evaluate its credibility, adapt it to context, act on it, and report what happened. Each transition can fail.

A publication is not implementation. A dataset is not understanding. A recommendation is not access. A service listing is not successful service delivery.

The missing work often lies in translation, coordination, trust, and feedback.

### Claim 3 — One interface cannot serve every context

A smartphone-first service may be convenient for one person and unusable for another. A desktop dashboard may be useful in an office but impractical in a field. An online community may support discovery but exclude people with limited connectivity, accessibility barriers, or a preference for face-to-face exchange.

Therefore, the shared layer should not require a shared front end.

### Claim 4 — Shared infrastructure can reduce repeated coordination costs

If observations, resources, opportunities, methods, and decisions can be represented in compatible, portable, and well-governed ways, people may spend less effort reconstructing context and more effort solving problems.

This is a testable proposition, not an assumed benefit. Shared infrastructure can also create new risks: surveillance, centralization, data extraction, cybersecurity exposure, bureaucratic overhead, or platform capture. Governance is therefore part of the technical design, not an afterthought.

### Claim 5 — Better coordination should be judged by human outcomes

More data, more engagement, more automation, and more platform usage are not sufficient measures of progress.

The relevant outcomes are whether a system improves health, safety, capability, economic resilience, environmental stewardship, access, agency, and meaningful participation—and whether it does so without imposing disproportionate costs on particular communities.

## 5. The proposed model: shared foundations, plural interfaces

MC proposes investigating a **participatory knowledge and capability infrastructure**.

This is not one giant database or a universal social network. It is a set of interoperable practices and tools that connect people, knowledge, resources, and projects while allowing different modes of participation.

### Shared foundations

1. **Portable records:** Information can move between tools and institutions in documented, usable formats.
2. **Provenance:** A person can inspect where a claim, measurement, recommendation, or record originated.
3. **Evidence distinctions:** Observation, testimony, interpretation, hypothesis, established finding, and decision remain distinguishable.
4. **Local context:** Place, time, conditions, constraints, and relevant community knowledge are preserved.
5. **Plural access:** Web, mobile, offline, telephone, print, in-person, and assistive routes can connect to the same underlying project where appropriate.
6. **Permission and control:** People understand what is collected, who can access it, how it is used, and how it can be corrected or withdrawn where applicable.
7. **Feedback:** Outcomes and failures can be reported back to improve the knowledge and the tools.
8. **Fair value exchange:** Contributors receive attribution, compensation, reciprocal benefit, or meaningful influence where appropriate.

### Different interfaces, compatible participation

- A farmer might contribute through an extension agent, field notebook, phone call, offline application, sensor, or web portal.
- A researcher might use structured datasets, methods, literature, and reproducible analysis.
- A social-media creator might translate findings into accessible stories, images, or public discussion without becoming the authority on the underlying science.
- A person who avoids smartphones might participate through local meetings, printed materials, telephone services, or a trusted intermediary.
- A public institution might publish verified resources and service pathways in formats that can be reused by other systems.

The common element is not a common device. It is the ability to preserve meaning, origin, context, rights, and feedback as information moves.

## 6. A practical example: a community soil and water problem

Consider a rural community concerned about declining soil health, water quality, or rising production costs.

A connected process could work as follows:

1. Producers describe observed conditions and operational constraints in the format they can realistically use.
2. Local extension staff help distinguish observations, measurements, and possible explanations.
3. Researchers contribute established methods and identify what additional evidence is needed.
4. Affordable tools or laboratories provide measurements with documented methods and limitations.
5. An analysis system compares observations while preserving location, time, provenance, and uncertainty.
6. Community members help determine which interventions are feasible and whose interests may be affected.
7. A pilot tests a limited intervention against a baseline or suitable comparison.
8. Results—including null or adverse results—are returned in plain language, technical formats, local meetings, and other accessible channels.
9. Participants decide whether to adapt, scale, or stop the intervention.

This example builds on existing extension and agricultural research structures. MC's potential contribution would be the continuity, evidence, and interoperability layer—not replacing agronomists, farmers, laboratories, or local decision-making.

The same architecture could be adapted to other domains, but each domain requires its own expertise, safeguards, and validation.

## 7. The strongest objections—and how the proposal must answer them

### Objection: "This is just another platform."

If the proposal depends on people joining one new destination, it risks reproducing the fragmentation it criticizes. MC should prioritize interoperable standards, exportability, open documentation, and integration with existing institutions. A public website can be an entrance, not the infrastructure itself.

### Objection: "This will surveil people and extract their data."

That risk is real. Data minimization, purpose limitation, meaningful consent, local control, access logs, retention limits, security review, and non-digital alternatives must be designed in from the beginning. Sensitive personal, medical, employment, and farm-operational data should not be made public merely because a project is public.

### Objection: "AI will replace local expertise."

It must not. AI can help organize information, surface questions, translate formats, and compare evidence. It cannot automatically know whether a recommendation fits a particular field, household, workplace, culture, or community. Human expertise and the right to challenge automated output must remain central.

### Objection: "The system will burden people who are already busy."

This is a decisive test. If participation creates more work than value, the design has failed. The system should reduce duplicate reporting, support low-friction contribution, return useful results, and compensate meaningful labor where appropriate.

### Objection: "There are already extension services, cooperatives, libraries, public institutions, and community organizations."

Correct. The proposal should not erase or duplicate them. It should map existing institutions, identify gaps, and test whether better interoperability and continuity can strengthen their work. Existing systems are assets and potential partners.

### Objection: "A national framework will ignore local differences."

A rigid national operating model would be inappropriate. Shared protocols should define minimum interoperability, rights, evidence, and accountability while leaving substantive priorities and implementation adaptable to local conditions.

### Objection: "This is too broad to test."

The full vision is broad; the first experiments must not be. Start with one place, one problem, a small number of participants, clear comparison conditions, and measurable outcomes. Expand only when evidence supports it.

## 8. What would count as success?

Success cannot be defined by sign-ups, screen time, number of records, AI usage, or the size of the repository alone.

A pilot should measure:

| Dimension | Possible measure |
|---|---|
| Access | Who can participate, through which channels, and who remains excluded |
| Burden | Time, cost, steps, repeated data entry, and travel required |
| Knowledge quality | Provenance completeness, correction rate, uncertainty disclosure, and reproducibility |
| Usefulness | Whether participants can find, understand, and apply relevant information |
| Agency | Whether people can refuse, correct, export, and control their contributions |
| Economic value | Costs avoided, time saved, income or resource effects where measurable |
| Social value | Trust, reciprocal benefit, participation, and quality of collaboration |
| Environmental value | Domain-specific indicators such as soil, water, energy, or waste |
| Distribution | Whether benefits and burdens differ by income, geography, age, disability, language, or connectivity |
| Durability | Whether improvements persist after the pilot and without constant external support |

Measures must be selected before an experiment begins. Baselines, comparison groups where feasible, adverse outcomes, missing data, and uncertainty must be reported. A successful demonstration in one community would not prove nationwide effectiveness.

## 9. A staged research and implementation program

### Phase 1 — Map the existing landscape
Identify existing platforms, public institutions, cooperative networks, extension services, libraries, community organizations, data standards, and service pathways. Document where handoffs fail and where successful bridges already exist.

**Deliverable:** A sourced system map and gap analysis.

### Phase 2 — Co-design with affected communities
Work with farmers, rural residents, people with disabilities, low-connectivity households, researchers, creators, public servants, and people who choose not to use smartphones. Do not treat any group as a monolith. Participants should help define the problem and what counts as benefit.

**Deliverable:** A community-authored requirements and governance specification.

### Phase 3 — Build a small interoperable prototype
Build one limited workflow with multiple input and output channels. Include provenance, permissions, export, offline operation where relevant, and an auditable change history.

**Deliverable:** A working prototype and documented threat model.

### Phase 4 — Run a bounded pilot
Choose one practical problem, such as a local agricultural knowledge pathway or a community resource coordination challenge. Define outcomes, comparison conditions, privacy safeguards, costs, and stopping rules in advance.

**Deliverable:** A reproducible pilot report, including failures and limitations.

### Phase 5 — Evaluate transferability
Test whether the model works in a second community with different conditions. Identify which components are generalizable and which must remain local.

**Deliverable:** A transferability assessment—not a claim of universal applicability.

### Phase 6 — Publish reusable infrastructure
Release non-sensitive schemas, methods, documentation, evaluation instruments, and software where licensing and security permit. Build partnerships rather than assuming MC should own every component.

**Deliverable:** Open, inspectable building blocks and a partnership roadmap.

## 10. The role of Mirror Cartographer

MC should serve as a research and continuity framework for investigating the relationships among:
- individual experience and institutional records;
- local knowledge and formal research;
- biological signals and human function;
- technology and sensory experience;
- data and decisions;
- public information and practical action;
- individual capability and collective outcomes.

Its distinctive discipline should be to preserve the difference between what is observed, what is inferred, what is proposed, what is tested, and what is learned.

MC should not claim to solve national coordination, replace existing institutions, or establish a new scientific field before evidence warrants those claims. Its contribution must be demonstrated through usable methods, transparent artifacts, partnerships, and evaluated pilots.

## Conclusion: progress as the quality of relationships

A society does not become more capable simply by adding more technology. It becomes more capable when people can use knowledge, tools, institutions, and one another to solve real problems without losing agency, context, or access.

The farmer should not have to become a content creator to make agricultural knowledge visible. The researcher should not have to guess what happens outside the laboratory. The creator should not have to become a scientist to communicate a discovery responsibly. The person who does not use a smartphone should not lose access to essential services. The person living with a disability should not be expected to adapt to every system without the system adapting in return.

Different people should be able to participate differently and still contribute to shared progress.

The ambition is not to make everyone live the same way. It is to build systems that allow different ways of living to coexist, exchange knowledge, solve problems, and share in the benefits of advancement.

**Technology should expand human capability without making human participation dependent on technology.**

That is the proposition Mirror Cartographer should investigate—and the proposition it must be prepared to test, revise, or reject in light of evidence.

## Open research questions

- Which forms of fragmentation create the greatest measurable harm in a chosen domain?
- Which existing institutions already solve parts of the problem, and what would strengthen rather than duplicate them?
- What minimum data and interoperability standards enable useful coordination without centralizing sensitive information?
- How can people contribute through non-digital channels without creating a second-class experience?
- How should communities govern, correct, license, or withdraw contributed information?
- What incentives fairly compensate people who contribute time, knowledge, data, or labor?
- Which outcomes can be measured credibly within a small pilot?
- Under what conditions does shared infrastructure make coordination worse rather than better?
