# Neural Degeneration, Neuroprotection & Regeneration
## A cross-disease research program for Alzheimer's disease, multiple sclerosis, ALS, and Parkinson's disease

**Mirror Cartographer — research architecture**  
**Originating project:** Charity Sturgell  
**Status:** Research map and program specification; not a clinical protocol  
**Version:** 1.0 · 30 October 2026

## 1. The central question

**How can we identify what is driving neural-system deterioration, interrupt that process, preserve surviving function, and restore biological structure or capability?**

This is not one problem. It is a sequence of linked problems:

1. Identify the initiating and sustaining causes of damage.
2. Detect the earliest measurable changes before irreversible loss.
3. Interrupt the damaging process without disabling necessary biological functions.
4. Protect cells, synapses, axons, myelin, glia, vasculature, and network function that remain.
5. Repair the local environment so endogenous repair becomes possible.
6. Replace cells or structures when repair is insufficient.
7. Reconnect repaired or replacement tissue to functioning circuits.
8. Measure whether the person has regained meaningful capability—not merely whether a biomarker changed.

The project must distinguish **disease modification**, **neuroprotection**, **repair**, **cell replacement**, **functional compensation**, and **rehabilitation**. They can reinforce one another, but none is proof of the others.

## 2. A common model of neural deterioration

A useful working model is a set of interacting layers, not a single linear cascade:

| Layer | What can fail | Research questions |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic / regulatory | Mutations, altered expression, RNA processing, epigenetic regulation | Which defects are causal, modifiable, and specific to a subgroup? |
| Protein quality control | Misfolding, aggregation, impaired degradation, trafficking failure | Which species are toxic, where do they act, and can clearance be restored safely? |
| Energy / mitochondria | ATP shortage, oxidative stress, impaired transport, metabolic inflexibility | Does energy failure precede cell death, and can the cell recover its energy balance? |
| Immune / inflammatory | Autoimmunity, chronic microglial activation, maladaptive astrocyte states, peripheral immune signaling | Which immune activity is harmful, protective, or context-dependent? |
| Membrane / electrical | Ion-channel dysfunction, altered excitability, synaptic transmission failure | Can electrical function be normalized without overexcitation or suppression? |
| Structural | Myelin loss, axonal injury, synapse loss, cytoskeletal disruption | What can be rebuilt, and what scaffolding or signals are required? |
| Vascular / clearance | Blood-brain barrier dysfunction, perfusion, perivascular and glymphatic clearance | Does impaired delivery or waste removal amplify pathology? |
| Network / systems | Circuit disconnection, compensation, maladaptive plasticity | Which circuits remain recruitable and how can they be retrained? |
| Functional / lived | Memory, movement, speech, sensation, fatigue, independence | Which biological changes translate into meaningful daily capability? |

These layers interact. For example, protein aggregation may impair mitochondria; energy failure may disrupt axonal transport; damaged tissue may activate glia; inflammation may then worsen synaptic and myelin injury. These relationships are hypotheses to test in each disease, not a universal causal chain.

## 3. The intervention ladder: from stopping damage to rebuilding function

### A. Remove or neutralize a driver
Examples: reduce a pathogenic protein, correct a disease-causing gene product, suppress a harmful immune response, or interrupt a toxic signaling pathway.

**Proof required:** target engagement, evidence the relevant pathology changed, and clinical outcomes that exceed placebo or natural-history expectations.

### B. Restore cellular housekeeping
Investigate proteostasis, autophagy-lysosome function, ubiquitin-proteasome pathways, RNA quality control, mitochondrial quality control (mitophagy), lipid handling, and intracellular transport.

**Key question:** can the cell regain the ability to maintain itself rather than requiring repeated external rescue?

### C. Restore metabolic and vascular support
Investigate mitochondrial function, NAD⁺ biology, glucose and lipid metabolism, oxygen delivery, neurovascular coupling, blood-brain barrier integrity, and waste clearance.

These are promising research domains, not permission to prescribe supplements or experimental metabolic interventions. A pathway that improves a laboratory measure may have no clinical benefit or may cause harm.

### D. Rebalance immune and glial activity
Microglia and astrocytes are not simply “good” or “bad.” Their functions change with context and time. Research should distinguish protective debris clearance and repair signaling from chronic inflammatory injury, and should test whether interventions preserve host defense and tissue maintenance.

### E. Repair myelin, axons, synapses, and circuits
Candidate directions include remyelination, oligodendrocyte precursor recruitment and maturation, axonal support, synaptic stabilization, neurotrophic signaling, activity-dependent plasticity, and rehabilitation paired with appropriate stimulation.

### F. Replace missing cells or functions
Potential strategies include stem-cell-derived neural progenitors, region-specific neuronal replacement, engineered tissues, gene or RNA delivery, and assistive interfaces. Cell survival is not enough: cells must mature, connect appropriately, receive inputs, produce regulated outputs, and avoid tumor formation, immune rejection, or maladaptive activity.

### G. Rebuild capability through closed-loop learning
Measure intention or residual function, decode it cautiously, deliver a cue or intervention, collect feedback, and adapt. This may restore useful capability even when biological regeneration is incomplete. It must be labelled as assistance or compensation unless tissue repair is independently demonstrated.

## 4. Four disease pathways

### 4.1 Alzheimer's disease — preserve synapses, reduce pathology, restore resilience

**Core pathology:** amyloid-beta accumulation, tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, neuronal and network loss, with variable contributions from vascular disease, inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and other co-pathologies.

**Research branches**
- Amyloid: production, aggregation, clearance, and the relationship between plaque removal and clinical benefit.
- Tau: spread, seeding, phosphorylation, intracellular trafficking, and tau-directed therapies.
- Synapses: synaptic density, plasticity, neurotransmission, and early circuit dysfunction.
- Cellular resilience: mitochondrial energy, NAD⁺, oxidative stress, protein quality control, and stress responses.
- Immune and glial biology: microglial states, complement-mediated synapse elimination, astrocyte function, and inflammatory timing.
- Clearance and vasculature: blood-brain barrier, perivascular drainage, glymphatic hypotheses, and vascular contributions.
- Neurogenesis and repair: determine whether adult human neurogenesis can be meaningfully increased and whether new neurons can integrate into useful circuits. Do not assume that increasing a neurogenesis marker restores memory.

**AI and measurement contribution**
- Combine validated blood / CSF biomarkers, imaging, cognitive measures, speech, gait, sleep, and daily-function observations where consented.
- Track each person's baseline and trajectory; separate disease signal from medication effects, sleep, mood, hearing, vision, vascular disease, and measurement noise.
- Identify subgroups and co-pathologies rather than treating all cognitive decline as one disease.
- Help select trial endpoints and detect discordance between biomarker response and functional response.

**Intervention classes to map**
- Approved anti-amyloid antibodies for selected people with early Alzheimer's and confirmed amyloid pathology; benefit is modest and treatment has important risks including ARIA.
- Tau-directed approaches, anti-inflammatory and immune-modulating strategies, synaptic / neurotrophic approaches, metabolic targets, and vascular / clearance targets remain at varying experimental stages.
- Cognitive, physical, sensory, sleep, and environmental support can preserve capability without being disease-modifying treatment.

**Regeneration question:** Can we preserve threatened synapses and neurons, restore synaptic function, and create conditions for circuit plasticity before extensive neuronal loss? If neurons are lost, can replacement cells integrate into memory networks? This is a much higher bar than generating new cells.

**Primary outcomes:** cognition, daily function, neuropsychiatric symptoms, caregiver burden, neurodegeneration markers, synaptic integrity, safety, and sustained quality of life.

### 4.2 Multiple sclerosis — stop immune injury, protect axons, remyelinate

**Core pathology:** immune-mediated CNS injury, demyelination, axonal damage, and neurodegeneration. Inflammation and neurodegeneration can coexist; progressive disability is not explained by relapses alone.

**Research branches**
- Peripheral immune drivers and CNS-compartmentalized inflammation.
- B cells, T cells, antibodies, microglia, and meningeal inflammation.
- Oligodendrocyte precursor cell recruitment, maturation, and remyelination failure.
- Axonal mitochondrial stress and energy demand after demyelination.
- Neurofilament light and other candidate markers of neuroaxonal injury.
- Chronic active lesions, lesion rims, and tissue repair limits.
- Activity-dependent myelin plasticity and rehabilitation.

**AI and measurement contribution**
- Integrate relapse history, MRI lesion activity, brain and spinal cord atrophy, neurofilament measures, gait, dexterity, vision, fatigue, cognition, and patient-reported function.
- Separate inflammatory activity from progression independent of relapse activity.
- Model within-person fluctuations (heat, fatigue, sleep, infection, medication timing) without treating every fluctuation as new tissue damage.
- Match rehabilitation and assistive feedback to residual function and changing fatigue.

**Intervention classes to map**
- Disease-modifying therapies that reduce inflammatory disease activity are established care, selected according to disease course and risk.
- Remyelination candidates and neuroprotective strategies are active research areas; no broadly established remyelinating cure exists.
- Functional electrical stimulation can assist selected functions such as foot clearance; BCI + FES rehabilitation is experimental and does not itself prove remyelination.
- Rehabilitation, mobility aids, symptom management, and energy-conservation strategies improve function but are not equivalent to stopping the underlying disease.

**Regeneration question:** Can we make the lesion environment permissive for oligodendrocyte maturation and durable remyelination while protecting axons from energy failure and chronic inflammation?

**Primary outcomes:** new / enlarging lesions, relapse rate, disability progression, MRI atrophy, neurofilament measures, remyelination-sensitive imaging, walking, hand function, vision, fatigue, cognition, and patient-valued activity.

### 4.3 ALS — protect motor neurons and preserve intention-to-action pathways

**Core pathology:** progressive upper and lower motor neuron degeneration. Genetic causes include SOD1, C9orf72, FUS, and TARDBP; most cases are sporadic, with TDP-43 pathology implicated in a large majority.

**Research branches**
- Genotype-specific drivers and precision treatment.
- TDP-43 dysfunction, RNA processing, cryptic splicing, STMN2 and UNC13A pathways.
- C9orf72 repeat-associated toxicity, RNA foci, and dipeptide-repeat proteins.
- Axonal transport, neuromuscular junction stability, excitotoxicity, mitochondrial stress, and proteostasis.
- Astrocyte, microglial, and peripheral immune contributions.
- Muscle and motor-unit preservation; distinguish primary muscle effects from denervation.
- Neural interfaces that preserve communication as motor output declines.

**AI and measurement contribution**
- Combine genetics, neurofilament light, motor-unit measures, respiratory function, speech, swallowing, strength, and functional scales.
- Track rate of change and identify measurement noise, compensatory strategies, and treatment response.
- Support patient-specific iPSC motor-neuron models and computational prioritization of candidate interventions.
- Decode attempted speech or residual neural signals into communication only with explicit consent, uncertainty handling, and user control.

**Intervention classes to map**
- Tofersen is an approved antisense therapy for SOD1-ALS; it is genotype-specific and not a general ALS treatment.
- Riluzole and edaravone are established treatment options with different evidence and eligibility considerations.
- RNA therapies, gene delivery, iPSC-based drug discovery, cell therapies, and immune modulation are investigational or early-stage in most contexts.
- AAC, eye gaze, speech-generating devices, and experimental BCIs preserve communication; they do not halt motor-neuron loss.

**Regeneration question:** Can we prevent motor-neuron death early enough, preserve neuromuscular connections, restore disrupted RNA / protein homeostasis, or replace motor neurons and reconnect them to muscle? The last goal is exceptionally difficult: a replacement neuron must establish long-distance projections, correct synapses, and controlled neuromuscular output.

**Primary outcomes:** survival, respiratory function, ALSFRS-R trajectory, strength, speech intelligibility, swallowing, communication rate and accuracy, quality of life, neurofilament, and treatment safety.

### 4.4 Parkinson's disease — protect dopamine circuits and restore distributed motor networks

**Core pathology:** degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta and dysfunction of basal-ganglia circuits. Alpha-synuclein pathology is central to many cases, but disease biology is heterogeneous and can involve non-dopaminergic systems. Motor symptoms include bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and postural instability; non-motor features can include sleep, autonomic, mood, sensory, and cognitive changes.

**Research branches**
- Alpha-synuclein: misfolding, aggregation, seeding, spread, clearance, and whether particular species are causally toxic.
- Lysosomal and autophagy pathways, including GBA-related biology.
- LRRK2 signaling and genotype-specific disease mechanisms.
- Mitochondrial quality control, PINK1 / Parkin pathways, oxidative stress, and cellular energy.
- Neuroinflammation and neuron-glia interactions.
- Dopamine neuron vulnerability, axonal arbor maintenance, and synaptic function.
- Gut-brain and peripheral nervous-system hypotheses, tested without assuming one route explains all cases.
- Non-dopaminergic pathology and the limits of dopamine replacement for gait, balance, cognition, and autonomic symptoms.

**AI and measurement contribution**
- Quantify gait, turning, freezing, tremor, bradykinesia, speech, sleep, and fluctuations in medication response using validated sensors and clinical assessment.
- Distinguish medication ON/OFF effects from disease progression and measurement artifacts.
- Integrate genotype, alpha-synuclein seed-amplification assays where validated, imaging, and clinical phenotype for stratification.
- Adapt external cueing and rehabilitation to an individual's movement pattern; retain clinician and user oversight.

**Intervention classes to map**
- Dopaminergic medication and deep-brain stimulation can substantially improve selected symptoms but are not established cures or general disease-stopping treatments.
- Disease-modifying strategies targeting alpha-synuclein, LRRK2, GBA / lysosomal pathways, inflammation, and mitochondrial function remain under investigation.
- Dopaminergic cell replacement is a major regenerative direction. A 2026 phase 1/2 open-label trial of human embryonic stem-cell-derived dopaminergic progenitors reported 12-month safety and interim outcomes in eight participants. This is early evidence; durability, efficacy, optimal patient selection, immunosuppression, and long-term safety require larger controlled studies.
- Rehabilitation, cueing, exercise, speech therapy, and assistive technology can improve function without demonstrating neuronal regeneration.

**Regeneration question:** Can transplanted dopamine-producing cells survive, mature, release dopamine in a regulated manner, integrate with host circuitry, and produce durable benefit? Can endogenous neurons be protected or their axonal arbor repaired before replacement is necessary? Could cell replacement be combined with treatments that slow ongoing pathology, so new cells are not exposed to the same damaging environment?

**Primary outcomes:** MDS-UPDRS, motor fluctuations, gait and freezing, falls, cognition, sleep, autonomic function, quality of life, medication burden, imaging / biomarker evidence, graft survival and safety.

## 5. The cross-disease research matrix

| Strategy | Alzheimer's | MS | ALS | Parkinson's |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Remove disease driver | Amyloid / tau targets | Pathogenic immune activity | Genetic / RNA / protein drivers | Alpha-synuclein, LRRK2, GBA pathways |
| Restore cell maintenance | Proteostasis, mitochondria, metabolism | Axonal energy, glial support | RNA processing, proteostasis, mitochondria | Lysosome, mitophagy, mitochondria |
| Rebalance immunity | Microglia / complement / inflammation | Peripheral and CNS immune activity | Glial and immune contributions | Neuroinflammation |
| Repair structure | Synapses, circuits, vascular support | Myelin, axons | Neuromuscular junction, motor pathways | Dopamine neurons, axons, circuits |
| Replace cells | Memory-circuit integration challenge | Oligodendrocyte / precursor strategies | Motor neuron replacement challenge | Dopaminergic progenitor replacement |
| Restore function | Memory aids, adaptive environments | FES, BCI, mobility rehabilitation | AAC, speech BCI, environmental control | DBS, cueing, rehabilitation, adaptive devices |

The matrix is a map of research directions, not evidence that all strategies are clinically validated in each disease.

## 6. The most promising cross-disease questions

1. **Cellular energy:** Is mitochondrial dysfunction an upstream driver, a downstream consequence, or both in specific disease subtypes? Which interventions restore function without shifting harmful metabolic burden elsewhere?
2. **Proteostasis:** Can autophagy, lysosomal function, and protein clearance be restored selectively without disrupting normal protein functions?
3. **Immune precision:** Can harmful immune activity be reduced while preserving protective debris clearance, infection defense, and repair signaling?
4. **Glial repair:** What makes microglia and astrocytes support regeneration in one context but sustain injury in another?
5. **Axonal maintenance:** Can long axons be protected by restoring mitochondrial distribution, transport, and local energy supply?
6. **Myelin and insulation:** Which signals allow oligodendrocyte precursors to mature and remyelinate chronically damaged axons?
7. **Synaptic resilience:** Can threatened synapses be stabilized before neurons die?
8. **Regenerative integration:** What prevents new cells from becoming functional members of a mature neural circuit?
9. **Disease environment:** Will a graft or regenerated cell survive if the pathological environment remains unchanged?
10. **Timing:** Which intervention works only before a threshold of irreversible damage, and how can that window be detected?
11. **Heterogeneity:** Can patients be grouped by mechanism rather than diagnosis alone?
12. **Meaningful recovery:** Which biological endpoints reliably predict improvements in movement, communication, cognition, independence, and lived experience?

## 7. Where AI can actually accelerate discovery

AI does not directly regenerate tissue. Its potential contribution is to shorten the cycle from observation to testable mechanism to intervention.

### Research acceleration pipeline

1. **Evidence graph:** connect genes, proteins, cell types, pathways, biomarkers, interventions, trial results, and functional outcomes with source provenance.
2. **Mechanism comparison:** identify shared pathways and disease-specific differences; prevent superficial similarity from becoming a false universal theory.
3. **Patient-derived models:** help analyze iPSC-derived neurons, glia, organoids, and co-culture experiments; prioritize candidate perturbations.
4. **Multimodal measurement:** integrate imaging, omics, electrophysiology, fluid biomarkers, wearables, and clinical scales while retaining modality-specific uncertainty.
5. **Target prioritization:** rank candidate mechanisms by causal evidence, tractability, safety, disease stage, and ability to measure target engagement.
6. **Experiment design:** propose falsifiable perturbation studies, controls, negative controls, dose ranges, and stopping rules for expert review.
7. **Adaptive trials:** support stratification and multi-arm / multi-stage trial design where statistically and ethically justified.
8. **Translation:** track whether a biological change produces durable improvement in a person's actual capability.

### Non-negotiable AI boundaries
- AI-generated hypotheses are not discoveries until tested.
- Retrospective prediction is not prospective clinical validity.
- A model must not infer intention, pain, cognition, or disease state as fact from weak proxies.
- Training data provenance, subgroup performance, calibration, missingness, and external validation must be visible.
- Clinical actions require qualified oversight, informed consent, safety review, and regulatory compliance.

## 8. How MC should expedite the work

MC's practical advantage should be **research coordination and evidence continuity**, not pretending to be a laboratory or clinical trial.

Build the following assets:

- **Mechanism graph:** entities and relationships with evidence grade, species, tissue, cell type, disease stage, and causal status.
- **Intervention ledger:** target, modality, mechanism, stage, eligibility, endpoint, safety signal, and status (approved / clinical / preclinical / speculative).
- **Evidence ledger:** primary source, publication date, study design, sample size, population, controls, outcomes, limitations, conflicts, and retraction / update status.
- **Contradiction register:** findings that disagree, alternative explanations, and experiments that could discriminate between them.
- **Biomarker-to-function map:** show which biomarkers measure target engagement, pathology, neuronal injury, or lived function; never collapse these into one outcome.
- **Research question queue:** each question has a falsifiable hypothesis, minimum evidence threshold, and next experiment.
- **Trial translation map:** connect preclinical evidence to human feasibility, safety, target engagement, efficacy, and real-world access.
- **Researcher interface:** allow experts and affected people to inspect, correct, challenge, and contribute without exposing private records.

## 9. Evidence grading

Use these labels for every claim:

- **E0 — Speculative:** plausible idea without direct supporting evidence.
- **E1 — Mechanistic / in vitro:** cellular or biochemical evidence; may not translate to an organism.
- **E2 — Animal / preclinical:** organism-level evidence; human translation uncertain.
- **E3 — Early human:** observational, feasibility, or phase 1 / early phase 2 evidence; usually not efficacy proof.
- **E4 — Controlled clinical evidence:** adequately controlled human evidence with clinically relevant endpoints; replication and generalizability still matter.
- **E5 — Established clinical use:** supported by regulatory approval or authoritative clinical guidance for a defined indication. This does not mean cure or universal benefit.

Each record must also state whether it is **direct evidence**, **indirect evidence**, **analogy**, or **unverified claim**. Grade evidence per claim, not per technology.

## 10. The experiments that could most efficiently reduce uncertainty

### Workstream A — shared mechanism atlas
Create a curated graph for protein quality control, mitochondria, immune / glial signaling, vascular / clearance biology, synapses, axons, and repair. First output: a set of high-confidence and disputed edges with sources.

### Workstream B — disease-specific mechanism maps
For each disease, identify the most validated causal mechanisms, the most tractable intervention points, the most informative biomarkers, and the major failure modes of past trials.

### Workstream C — regeneration bottleneck analysis
For each tissue target, map the sequence: cell source → survival → maturation → migration / positioning → integration → regulated function → durable clinical benefit. Identify where evidence breaks.

### Workstream D — biomarker-to-capability validation
Ask whether a change in a molecular or imaging marker predicts a meaningful functional change. Include discordant cases rather than excluding them.

### Workstream E — AI-assisted evidence synthesis
Build source-grounded retrieval and claim comparison. Require citations, uncertainty, and human review. Start with public literature and synthetic examples; do not upload private health records to public systems.

### Workstream F — translational partnerships
Identify university labs, patient-led research groups, clinical investigators, rehabilitation teams, and responsible industry partners. MC can contribute research maps, synthesis, reproducible analysis, and research questions; wet-lab experiments and clinical studies require qualified institutions, funding, ethics review, and regulatory approval.

## 11. First 90-day plan

**Days 1–15: Evidence foundation**
- Build source register for the four diseases.
- Define shared mechanism vocabulary and evidence grades.
- Identify approved therapies versus investigational interventions.
- Capture key failed and negative trials, not only promising results.

**Days 16–35: Disease maps**
- Build four disease pathway maps and a shared mechanism graph.
- Map target → intervention → biomarker → functional endpoint.
- Record evidence gaps and contradictory results.

**Days 36–55: Regeneration bottlenecks**
- Compare remyelination, synaptic repair, axonal support, motor-neuron replacement, and dopaminergic cell replacement.
- Identify what is biologically feasible now versus speculative.
- Define safety and integration requirements.

**Days 56–75: AI research prototype**
- Build a source-linked query and evidence comparison workflow.
- Test whether it can identify contradictions and missing links better than a simple literature table.
- Evaluate citation accuracy and expert correction burden.

**Days 76–90: Public research release**
- Publish the atlas with clear evidence labels.
- Invite external review from neuroscientists, clinicians, rehabilitation specialists, and patient communities.
- Select one narrow, tractable pilot question for deeper work.

## 12. What success would mean

MC should not measure success by the number of connections in a graph or the novelty of a theory.

A useful success ladder is:

1. A question is made precise.
2. Relevant evidence is found and traceable.
3. Competing mechanisms are represented fairly.
4. A key uncertainty is reduced.
5. A testable experiment is designed.
6. An independent team can reproduce or challenge it.
7. A target is validated in an appropriate model.
8. A safe human study demonstrates target engagement.
9. A controlled study shows clinically meaningful benefit.
10. The intervention reaches people equitably and improves lived capability.

**The long-term aim:** preserve what is still alive, repair what can be repaired, replace what cannot be restored, and reconnect biological systems to the capabilities and relationships that make a life one's own.
