# Mirror Cartographer — Independent Researcher Field Guide
**Audience:** Anyone using MC to investigate a question independently  
**Status:** Practical method guide; it describes a workflow, not a claim that every step is automated in the software  
**Purpose:** Help a researcher move from curiosity to a traceable, challengeable, evidence-bounded conclusion.

## The promise

Mirror Cartographer is a way to organize inquiry so that a researcher can see:
- what prompted a question;
- what is directly observed versus inferred;
- which explanations compete;
- what evidence supports or contradicts each explanation;
- what remains unknown;
- what test would distinguish the explanations;
- why a conclusion changed.

MC should not decide what is true by making a story coherent. It should make the route to a conclusion inspectable and make it easier to notice when the evidence does not justify a conclusion.

## 1. Start with the question, not a theory

Write the question in one sentence. Then record:
- **Phenomenon:** What happened or was observed?
- **Scope:** Which people, organisms, systems, dates, environments, or cases are included?
- **Scale:** Molecular, cellular, tissue, organismal, social, technical, or another level?
- **Outcome:** What exactly would count as an answer?
- **Motivation:** Why is this worth investigating? Keep personal meaning separate from evidence.
- **Constraints:** Time, tools, expertise, access, ethics, privacy, and budget.

Avoid beginning with a preferred explanation. “Why does X happen?” is usually a better starting point than “How does my theory of X work?”

## 2. Split the inquiry into distinct record types

Do not store everything as prose in one document. Keep these categories distinct and link them:

1. **Observation:** What was directly seen, measured, reported, or recorded?
2. **Source:** Where did the observation or claim come from?
3. **Interpretation:** What might the observation mean?
4. **Claim:** A sentence that can be assessed as true, false, partly true, or currently unresolvable.
5. **Hypothesis:** A possible explanation that predicts something observable.
6. **Alternative:** Another explanation that could produce the same observation.
7. **Evidence record:** A study, dataset, document, experiment, or testimony relevant to a claim.
8. **Unknown:** What is not currently known or cannot be inferred from the available evidence.
9. **Test:** A search, comparison, calculation, interview, experiment, or analysis that could change the conclusion.
10. **Decision/revision:** What changed, why it changed, and which evidence justified the change.
11. **Symbol/metaphor:** A way to express or explore meaning. It can generate questions, but is not scientific evidence.
12. **Action:** A next step, with its owner, prerequisites, risk, and completion criteria.

Never silently promote an interpretation into an observation or a hypothesis into a fact.

## 3. Build a question tree

For each main question, generate smaller questions under separate headings:
- **Mechanism:** How could this happen?
- **Measurement:** How would we know it happened?
- **Timing:** What occurs before, during, and after?
- **Context:** Under what conditions does it occur or fail to occur?
- **Alternatives:** What else could explain it?
- **Counterexamples:** Where does the proposed explanation fail?
- **Prior art:** Who has already studied it, under what terms?
- **Implications:** What follows if it is true—and what does not follow?
- **Actionability:** What could be tested or changed safely?

Keep branches separate when they ask different questions. Link them when a specific relationship can be stated and justified. A connection without a defined relationship is a lead, not a finding.

## 4. Search the literature and the counter-literature

Search using:
- the exact phenomenon;
- likely technical terms and synonyms;
- mechanism names;
- alternative explanations;
- “negative result,” “failed replication,” “limitations,” “null,” “contradiction,” “expression of concern,” “correction,” and “retraction” where relevant.

For each source, record:
- full citation and stable locator;
- primary study, review, guideline, dataset, commentary, or anecdote;
- date and source status;
- population/species, sample, setting, and scale;
- method, intervention, comparator, and outcome;
- result and uncertainty;
- stated limitations and plausible bias;
- which exact claim it supports—and which it does not support.

A review helps map a field; it is not an independent replication of every study it cites. Multiple pages repeating one paper are not multiple independent evidence sources.

## 5. Use the perspective rotation

Assign distinct review jobs. One person can rotate through these roles for a small project; for consequential work, recruit genuinely independent reviewers.

- **Builder:** Make the strongest fair version of the proposed explanation.
- **Domain specialist:** Check whether the mechanism matches the field.
- **Measurement reviewer:** Check whether the evidence measures the claimed construct.
- **Causal reviewer:** Check confounding, selection, reverse causation, and alternative explanations.
- **Prior-art reviewer:** Search for existing versions of the idea.
- **Adversary:** Try to break the explanation using counterexamples.
- **Ethics/privacy reviewer:** Check potential harm, consent, and sensitive information.
- **Verifier:** Match every claim to the exact evidence and scope.
- **Cartographer:** Preserve sources, chronology, contradictions, and revisions.

These are functions, not personalities that magically confer expertise. Simulated role rotation is useful for finding questions, but it is not independent expert review.

## 6. Use the evidence-to-claim ladder

Suggested statuses:
- **Unexamined:** A claim has been proposed but not checked.
- **Question:** Worth investigating; not yet a factual assertion.
- **Plausible hypothesis:** Has a coherent mechanism or some supporting evidence, but key alternatives remain.
- **Supported in a defined context:** Evidence supports the claim within a stated population, model, or condition.
- **Replicated / convergent:** Multiple genuinely independent lines of evidence support the bounded claim.
- **Operationally validated:** The method performs against prespecified tests for its intended use.
- **Disconfirmed / rejected:** Evidence conflicts with the claim or its predictions fail.
- **Unresolved:** Evidence is insufficient or conflicting.

Always state scope. “Shown in a mouse model” must not become “shown in humans.” “Associated with” must not become “causes.” “A marker changed” must not become “the system recovered.”

## 7. Make hypotheses falsifiable

Use this compact template:

- **Hypothesis:** If X is true, then Y should occur under condition Z.
- **Mechanism:** Why would X produce Y?
- **Prediction:** What observable result follows?
- **Comparator:** Compared with what?
- **Alternatives:** What else could produce the same result?
- **Disconfirmation:** What result would make us reject or narrow the hypothesis?
- **Measurement:** What is measured directly and what is only a proxy?
- **Scope:** Where should the hypothesis apply, and where might it not?
- **Next test:** The smallest informative action.
- **Status change rule:** What evidence would promote, demote, or close this claim?

If no conceivable result would change the researcher's mind, the statement is not functioning as a testable hypothesis.

## 8. Keep the timeline

Preserve the evolution of the investigation:
- original question;
- first interpretation;
- alternative explanations;
- evidence added;
- challenge or contradiction;
- revised interpretation;
- reason for revision;
- remaining uncertainty.

Do not overwrite the earlier claim when it changes. Mark it as superseded and link the new version. The revision history can reveal how the idea developed and whether a conclusion changed because of evidence or merely because the conversation became more persuasive.

## 9. Keep meaning and evidence connected but separate

Personal experience, metaphor, intuition, art, and analogy can be valuable discovery tools. They can point toward an unasked question or help explain why a question matters.

For each symbolic connection, record:
- the image or analogy;
- the insight it suggests;
- the literal claim it might tempt you to make;
- what would be needed to test that literal claim;
- where the analogy breaks.

A resemblance between two phenomena is not proof that they share a mechanism. Preserve the inspiration without letting it substitute for evidence.

## 10. A worked abstract example

**Observation:** A system appears to return to its previous measured state after a perturbation.

**Interpretation A:** It recovered fully.

**Alternative B:** The measured variable returned to baseline, but reserve or another unmeasured function remains impaired.

**Alternative C:** The sampling interval missed a delayed effect.

**Tests:**
1. Measure several independent functional outcomes over time.
2. Apply a second standardized challenge after apparent recovery.
3. Compare with an unperturbed control and a suitable reference range.
4. Check whether the measurement can distinguish the competing states.
5. Predefine what counts as recovery and what would disconfirm each explanation.

**Lesson:** One visible marker cannot stand in for the full hidden state. The same logic applies in biology, engineering, education, social systems, and personal research, although each field needs its own valid measurements.

## 11. Practical session checklist

At the end of each research session, answer:
- What did I learn that I did not know at the start?
- Which claim became stronger, weaker, narrower, or was rejected?
- What is the strongest counterexample found?
- What is the most important unknown?
- Which source most directly supports the main claim?
- Which source most directly challenges it?
- Did I confuse correlation with causation, proxy with construct, or analogy with mechanism?
- What is the next test that would most reduce uncertainty?
- What should be kept private or excluded from the public record?

## 13. What MC can and cannot promise

MC can provide a disciplined structure for recording questions, evidence, competing explanations, uncertainty, and revisions. Where repository tools or other software are available, some of this can be automated or linked.

MC should not promise:
- that a coherent map is true;
- that a model's simulated reviewer roles equal independent experts;
- that a search is exhaustive unless its scope and procedure support that claim;
- that a hypothesis is novel before prior-art review;
- that an artifact's presence in a repository proves its scientific validity;
- that a result in one species, population, or setting automatically generalizes;
- that research replaces professional, ethical, or clinical review where required.

The user remains the investigator and decision-maker. The system's job is to make reasoning easier to inspect, challenge, revise, and reuse.

## 14. Recommended starting sequence

For a first project:
1. Write one question and define its scope.
2. Create 5–10 subquestions.
3. Search for prior art and at least one serious alternative explanation.
4. Build a source ledger before drafting a conclusion.
5. Write 1–3 falsifiable hypotheses.
6. Ask the adversary to find counterexamples.
7. Define one small, ethical, feasible test.
8. Record results and uncertainty.
9. Revise the claim and preserve the history.
10. Publish only the bounded conclusion, method, sources, and limitations that can be defended.
