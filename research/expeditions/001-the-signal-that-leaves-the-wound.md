# Expedition 001 — The Signal That Leaves the Wound

**Project:** Mirror Cartographer  
**Status:** Initial evidence-grounded research dossier; not a systematic review  
**Created:** 2026-11-01  
**Research question:** How do living systems encode, propagate, limit, and interpret information about injury—and can the dynamics of those signals help distinguish local repair, systemic defense, and regenerative patterning?

> **Scope boundary:** This dossier compares mechanisms and research designs. It does not establish a universal wound-signaling pathway, a treatment, or a cure. Plant defense, epithelial wound closure, and whole-body regeneration are different biological outcomes.

## 1. Why this question is worth investigating

An injury is local. The response often is not.

A damaged cell can release extracellular molecules; neighboring cells can change intracellular calcium; plant tissues can transmit electrical, calcium, reactive-oxygen, and hydraulic signals to distant organs; regenerative animals can use early physiological states to influence later body patterning.

This creates a tractable cross-domain question: **what information is carried by an injury signal, how far does it travel, how is it stopped, and what determines the response of the receiving tissue?**

The potentially consequential idea is not that all organisms use the same mechanism. It is that repair outcomes may depend on a multi-layer signaling architecture whose dynamics can be measured and compared.

## 2. Evidence map: observations that anchor the investigation

### A. Animal epithelial injury: ATP can initiate a local calcium wave

**Handly & Wollman (2017), Molecular Biology of the Cell**  
[Wound-induced Ca2+ wave propagates through a simple release and diffusion mechanism](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5449146/) · [DOI 10.1091/mbc.e16-10-0695](https://doi.org/10.1091/mbc.e16-10-0695)

- **System / method:** Mechanically wounded epithelial monolayers, microfluidic wounding assay, manipulation of ATP release and ATP hydrolysis, calcium imaging.
- **Reported result:** Extracellular ATP released from wounded cells activates purinergic receptors and a calcium response in surrounding cells. The experiments supported release and diffusion as sufficient to explain the propagation pattern tested.
- **Important boundary:** This is a defined epithelial culture model and a local wound response. It does not establish how systemic signals operate in intact organs or how regeneration is specified.
- **Why it matters:** A wave-like calcium pattern does not by itself imply a self-propagating intracellular relay. Extracellular release and diffusion can produce a spatially organized response.

### B. Plant injury: glutamate-linked channels participate in long-distance signaling

**Mousavi et al. (2013), Nature**  
[GLUTAMATE RECEPTOR-LIKE genes mediate leaf-to-leaf wound signalling](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12478) · [DOI 10.1038/nature12478](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12478)

- **System / method:** Arabidopsis thaliana; non-invasive electrode recordings and analysis of systemic jasmonate responses after leaf wounding.
- **Reported result:** GLR3.3 and GLR3.6 participate in wound-induced electrical signaling between leaves and in distal defense responses.
- **Boundary:** Plant GLR proteins are homologous to animal ionotropic glutamate receptors, but this does not mean plant and animal signaling systems are interchangeable or that plants possess a nervous system.
- **Why it matters:** A receptor/channel family can be evolutionarily related while operating in a very different tissue architecture and organism-level function.

**Toyota et al. (2018), Science**  
[Glutamate triggers long-distance, calcium-based plant defense signaling](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30213912/) · [DOI 10.1126/science.aat7744](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aat7744)

- **System / method:** Wounded Arabidopsis leaves, glutamate application, calcium imaging and defense-response measurements.
- **Reported result:** Wound-released glutamate activates GLR-family channels, triggering calcium increases that propagate to distant organs and induce defense responses.
- **Boundary:** This is plant systemic defense, not animal tissue regeneration.
- **Why it matters:** It supplies a mechanistic bridge between local damage chemistry, membrane channel activity, calcium dynamics, and distal response.

**Fichman et al. (2021), The Plant Journal**  
[Integration of electric, calcium, reactive oxygen species and hydraulic signals during rapid systemic signaling in plants](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/tpj.15360) · [DOI 10.1111/tpj.15360](https://doi.org/10.1111/tpj.15360)

- **System / method:** Arabidopsis wild type and signaling mutants; simultaneous imaging/measurement of membrane potential, calcium, ROS, and hydraulic pressure under matched growth and stress conditions.
- **Reported result:** Wounding and high-light stress recruited overlapping but non-identical systemic signal dependencies. GLR3.3/GLR3.6, PDLP5 and PIP2;1 contributed to particular components; the hierarchy differed by stimulus.
- **Boundary:** The result argues against treating the signals as one undifferentiated wave. It is specific to the tested species, developmental stage, conditions, and stressors.
- **Why it matters:** It provides an explicit precedent for measuring multiple signal channels together and testing their dependencies rather than inferring a pathway from one readout.

**Fichman et al. (2021), Plant Physiology**  
[Vascular and nonvascular transmission of systemic reactive oxygen signals during wounding and heat stress](https://academic.oup.com/plphys/article/186/3/1721/6211198) · [DOI 10.1093/plphys/kiab157](https://doi.org/10.1093/plphys/kiab157)

- **Reported result:** In Arabidopsis, ROS signals could propagate through mesophyll cells during wounding and heat stress; mesophyll ROS production contributed to systemic wound-response and acclimation transcript accumulation.
- **Boundary:** Not all stressors produced the same propagation behavior. The authors distinguish wounding, heat, and high-light responses.
- **Why it matters:** The route through tissue architecture is part of the signal, not a trivial detail.

**Li et al. (2023), Nature Plants**  
[Ca2+/calmodulin-mediated desensitization of glutamate receptors shapes plant systemic wound signalling and anti-herbivore defence](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41477-023-01578-8)

- **Reported result:** Calcium/calmodulin-dependent desensitization of GLR channels acts as negative feedback. A CRISPR-engineered GLR3.3 allele with impaired desensitization prolonged systemic electrical and calcium signals and enhanced defense against herbivores in the tested plant system.
- **Boundary:** Enhanced defense against herbivores is not equivalent to improved wound healing or regeneration. The result does not justify directly manipulating homologous pathways in humans.
- **Why it matters:** Signal termination and feedback may be as consequential as signal initiation. A longer signal is not automatically a better signal.

### C. Regeneration: bioelectric state can influence pattern, not merely wound closure

**Adams et al. (2007), Developmental Biology**  
[H+ pump-dependent changes in membrane voltage are an early mechanism necessary and sufficient to induce Xenopus tail regeneration](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17329365/)

- **System / method:** Xenopus tail amputation; pharmacological and molecular-genetic manipulation of V-ATPase and membrane voltage.
- **Reported result:** V-ATPase activity and associated membrane-voltage changes were required for tail regeneration in the tested model, while wound healing and tail development were separable outcomes.
- **Boundary:** Amphibian larval regeneration is not adult human regeneration. Pharmacological perturbations can have context-dependent effects.
- **Why it matters:** It directly separates closure of a wound from successful regeneration of a structure.

**Beane et al. (2013), Development**  
[Bioelectric signaling regulates head and organ size during planarian regeneration](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23250205/) · [DOI 10.1242/dev.086900](https://doi.org/10.1242/dev.086900)

- **System / method:** Planarian regeneration; RNA interference of H+,K+-ATPase and analysis of regenerated anatomy.
- **Reported result:** Membrane-voltage-dependent signaling influenced head size and organ scaling. Manipulation altered the proportional organization of regenerated structures without simply eliminating blastema production.
- **Boundary:** Planarian regenerative biology is a model system; the result does not establish a direct therapeutic method in mammals.
- **Why it matters:** Regeneration requires control of size, position, and proportion—not just cell proliferation.

**Durant et al. (2019), Biophysical Journal**  
[The Role of Early Bioelectric Signals in the Regeneration of Planarian Anterior/Posterior Polarity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6401388/) · [DOI 10.1016/j.bpj.2019.01.029](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpj.2019.01.029)

- **System / method:** Brief manipulation of membrane voltage during the first three hours after planarian amputation, followed by gene-expression and anatomical analysis.
- **Reported result:** Transient early depolarization altered later gene expression and produced double-headed phenotypes after regeneration, despite washout of the ionophore.
- **Boundary:** A striking model-organism result; it does not mean voltage manipulation can safely or predictably regenerate human organs.
- **Why it matters:** The timing of a signal may matter: a short early perturbation can have delayed structural consequences.

### D. Human relevance: wound electric fields are measurable, but therapeutic claims remain bounded

**Sheridan et al. (1996), Journal of Investigative Dermatology**  
[Imposition of a physiologic DC electric field alters the migratory response of human keratinocytes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8617998/)

- **System / method:** Human keratinocytes exposed to imposed direct-current electric fields on extracellular-matrix substrates.
- **Reported result:** The substrate modified the migratory response to an electric field.
- **Boundary:** In vitro cell migration does not establish improved clinical healing from externally applied electrical stimulation.
- **Why it matters:** The receiving cell's environment can change how it interprets a signal.

**M Zhao et al. (2011), Journal of Investigative Dermatology**  
[The electric field near human skin wounds declines with age and provides a noninvasive indicator of wound healing](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22092802/)

- **System / method:** Non-invasive measurement of lateral electric fields adjacent to skin wounds in younger and older adults.
- **Reported result:** The measured field near wounds differed by age in the reported sample.
- **Boundary:** This is a measurement study, not proof that restoring a measured field reverses age-related healing impairment.
- **Why it matters:** It motivates testing whether bioelectric measurements can serve as a longitudinal biomarker, with validation against clinical outcomes.

## 3. Cross-domain comparison: what is genuinely shared?

| Feature | Epithelial wound model | Plant systemic defense | Planarian / Xenopus regeneration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial event | Cell/tissue damage | Mechanical injury/herbivory | Amputation |
| Candidate early signals | Extracellular ATP, purinergic activation, Ca2+ | Glutamate, GLRs, electrical change, Ca2+, ROS, hydraulic signals | Membrane voltage, ion transport, downstream gene regulation |
| Spatial scale shown | Local cell neighborhood | Distal leaves / organs | Wound region and body-axis patterning |
| Main outcome studied | Epithelial migration/closure | Systemic defense readiness | Regenerative polarity, structure, size |
| Critical control | Signal dilution/termination and receptor response | Channel activity, signal integration, tissue route, feedback | Timing and spatial pattern of bioelectric state |
| What must not be conflated | Calcium wave ≠ regeneration | Plant defense ≠ animal healing | Regeneration model ≠ human therapy |

**Established at a broad level:** Cells and tissues use chemical, ionic, electrical, and mechanical/pressure-related changes to coordinate responses to injury. The specific mediators and architecture differ.

**Not established:** One conserved master wound signal; a universal calcium-wave mechanism; a direct plant-to-human treatment translation; or a proven way to restore human regenerative capacity by manipulating bioelectric signals.

## 4. The research gap that appears most actionable

A recurring weakness across the literature is that signals are often measured separately, under different conditions, with different organisms, instruments, wound geometries, and time windows. Fichman et al. explicitly designed matched-condition measurements because earlier studies had been performed across different laboratories and conditions.

This creates a practical research opportunity:

**Build a time-resolved, multi-signal account of injury response that records signal onset, propagation, spatial reach, duration, feedback/termination, tissue context, and functional outcome in the same experimental system.**

The purpose is not to assume all signals form a single chain. It is to measure dependencies and test competing causal structures.

## 5. Initial hypotheses — explicitly provisional

### H1. Signal dynamics may predict response class better than peak amplitude alone

**Proposition:** The combination of onset latency, propagation speed, spatial reach, duration, and termination dynamics may distinguish local containment, systemic defense, and regenerative patterning better than maximum signal amplitude alone.

- **Basis:** Plant studies show stimulus-specific combinations and dependencies among electrical, calcium, ROS, and hydraulic signals; regeneration studies show early timing and voltage state can affect later pattern.
- **Alternative:** Signal dynamics may merely reflect tissue type, wound size, or developmental stage, with no general predictive value.
- **Disconfirmation:** In a defined model system, dynamic features do not improve out-of-sample prediction over simple injury and tissue-context variables.

### H2. Signal termination is an active control variable, not just signal decay

**Proposition:** Feedback and termination mechanisms may determine whether a response remains localized, becomes systemic, or produces damaging overactivation.

- **Basis:** GLR3.3 calcium/calmodulin desensitization in plants provides a concrete negative-feedback example; ATP diffusion/hydrolysis provides a different limiting mechanism in epithelial wound signaling.
- **Alternative:** Differences in duration are explained by release magnitude, geometry, degradation, or measurement thresholds without a distinct regulatory function.
- **Disconfirmation:** Selective perturbation of candidate termination mechanisms does not change response duration or downstream function after controlling for signal initiation.

### H3. Signal interpretation depends on tissue architecture and receiving-cell state

**Proposition:** The same broad signal class can produce different outcomes depending on tissue connectivity, extracellular matrix, receptor/channel state, and developmental context.

- **Basis:** Plant vascular/mesophyll routes, epithelial substrate-dependent galvanotaxis, and regenerative membrane-voltage patterning.
- **Alternative:** Apparent context effects are due to unmeasured differences in signal exposure or cell health.
- **Disconfirmation:** Under matched signal exposure, changing the proposed receiving context does not alter the response.

### H4. Repair and regeneration may diverge after the initial wound-response phase

**Proposition:** Early damage detection may be shared at a functional level, while later repair-versus-regeneration decisions depend on distinct positional, immune, metabolic, and developmental control systems.

- **Basis:** Xenopus studies distinguish wound healing from tail regeneration; planarian studies connect bioelectric state to anatomical polarity and scaling.
- **Alternative:** The apparent distinction is a continuum of response magnitude or duration rather than a distinct control transition.
- **Disconfirmation:** Matched perturbations and longitudinal outcomes fail to reveal separable early-response and pattern-restoration phases.

## 6. Proposed experiment ladder

These are research designs for qualified laboratories, not instructions for unsupervised biological experimentation.

### Experiment 1 — Reproduce and quantify a local epithelial signal

- **Model:** Established epithelial monolayer wound model.
- **Readouts:** Extracellular ATP dynamics, intracellular Ca2+ imaging, wound-edge migration/closure, cell viability.
- **Perturbations:** ATP scavenging and carefully controlled purinergic-pathway perturbations; vehicle and sham-wound controls.
- **Question:** Does the ATP-release/diffusion account predict signal range and closure under controlled changes in wound geometry?
- **Decision criterion:** Model must predict held-out spatial-temporal calcium profiles and functional closure, not merely fit the same data used to construct it.

### Experiment 2 — Measure plant systemic signals together, not one at a time

- **Model:** Arabidopsis, with wild type and justified pathway mutants/lines.
- **Readouts:** Membrane potential, Ca2+, ROS, hydraulic pressure, distal defense gene/hormone/metabolite readouts.
- **Perturbations:** Wounding versus heat/high-light controls; relevant GLR, RBOHD, PDLP5/PIP2;1 genotypes as supported by existing literature.
- **Question:** Which signal dependencies and temporal order are specific to the wound stimulus and tissue route?
- **Decision criterion:** Predefined causal-dependency predictions, biological replicates, blinded image quantification where feasible, and independent replication.

### Experiment 3 — Separate wound closure from pattern restoration

- **Model:** A regenerative model with established bioelectric perturbation methods (planarian or Xenopus, selected by specialist collaborators).
- **Readouts:** Early voltage state; downstream gene-expression polarity; wound closure; blastema formation; final anatomical pattern and function.
- **Perturbations:** Time-limited, reversible perturbations with sham and toxicity controls.
- **Question:** Which temporal windows affect closure, which affect polarity, and which affect final pattern?
- **Decision criterion:** Distinct outcomes must be measured separately. A closed wound is not counted as successful regeneration.

### Experiment 4 — Test the proposed predictive signature

- **Design:** Within each model separately, extract prespecified signal features: onset latency, propagation speed, spatial reach, peak, integrated signal, duration, recovery/termination, and tissue context.
- **Analysis:** Compare baseline predictors (injury size, tissue type, developmental stage) against baseline-plus-signal-dynamics models. Use held-out experiments, not random frame-level splits.
- **Question:** Do signal dynamics predict downstream outcome beyond basic injury/context variables?
- **Decision criterion:** Improvement must generalize to independently collected experiments. Cross-species prediction is a later question, not the first validation target.

## 7. What would make this a useful bridge to technology and agriculture?

The first technology contribution is measurement, not speculative stimulation.

- **Bioelectronics:** Flexible, low-noise electrodes and sensor arrays for mapping wound-associated voltage gradients; synchronized acquisition with optical calcium/ROS imaging where technically possible.
- **Imaging and computation:** Spatiotemporal segmentation, event detection, signal propagation models, uncertainty estimates, and reproducible analysis pipelines.
- **Materials science:** Test how substrate stiffness, conductivity, hydration, and extracellular-matrix properties alter signal reception and cell migration. Keep each variable experimentally separable.
- **Agriculture:** Investigate whether plant systemic signal dynamics predict distal defense readiness, crop damage response, or resilience under controlled stress. Do not assume a stronger or longer signal always improves yield or fitness.
- **Field translation:** Only after controlled validation, evaluate whether robust, low-cost sensing can measure meaningful plant stress or wound responses outside laboratory conditions.

## 8. The highest-value next questions

1. Which plant systemic-signal findings have been independently replicated across species, cultivars, growth conditions, and wound types?
2. How do ATP/purinergic epithelial calcium waves compare quantitatively with GLR-linked plant calcium waves in propagation range, speed, termination, and dependence on tissue connectivity?
3. Which animal regeneration systems show causal bioelectric control of patterning, and which findings survive independent replication?
4. What is the relationship between early damage signaling, inflammation, fibrosis/scarring, and successful regeneration in mammalian tissues?
5. Can signal dynamics be measured non-invasively and linked to clinically meaningful healing outcomes?
6. Which sensor technologies can capture voltage, ionic, chemical, and mechanical signals simultaneously without materially perturbing the system?

## 9. What this dossier does not yet contain

- A systematic search protocol or complete PRISMA-style study selection.
- A formal risk-of-bias assessment for every included paper.
- Independent replication grading for every result.
- A complete map of negative/null studies, retractions, corrections, and unpublished work.
- A validated cross-species quantitative model.
- A validated therapeutic target or treatment recommendation.
- Experimental data generated by Mirror Cartographer.

These are not footnotes. They define the next work.

## 10. MC proof-engine requirements exposed by this expedition

A functioning proof engine must be able to:

1. Preserve each paper as a source record with DOI/PMID, publication type, model, methods, measured variables, result, limitations, and correction/retraction status.
2. Extract claims separately from papers and bind each claim to exact supporting passages or figures.
3. Distinguish primary research, review, preprint, protocol, commentary, and clinical evidence.
4. Compare methods and biological contexts before asserting cross-study agreement.
5. Identify negative, contradictory, and null findings—not only supportive papers.
6. Track replication, independent groups, reused datasets, and citation lineage.
7. Represent relationships with typed edges: same mechanism, homologous component, shared function, analogy, correlation, causal perturbation, or unresolved.
8. Require a counter-hypothesis and a disconfirmation criterion for generated hypotheses.
9. Generate experiments with controls, measurable variables, analysis plans, and explicit decision criteria.
10. Preserve version history so revisions do not erase why an earlier interpretation was considered plausible.

## 11. Current conclusion

The evidence supports a serious research direction: injury responses involve dynamic, context-dependent signaling; signal propagation and feedback differ across biological systems; and bioelectric state can influence regenerative patterning in some model organisms.

The strongest opportunity is to investigate **how signal dynamics, tissue architecture, and feedback jointly shape the transition from damage detection to functional repair or regeneration**.

The strongest caution is equally important: shared words such as calcium wave, glutamate receptor, ROS, and electrical signal can conceal very different mechanisms and outcomes.

**Current status:** Research question established; initial source cluster assembled; provisional hypotheses and experiment ladder specified. No novel biological discovery has been established. The next phase is a systematic evidence audit, including contradictory and negative findings, followed by a reproducible quantitative comparison in selected model systems.
