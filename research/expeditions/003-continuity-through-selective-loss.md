# Expedition 003 — Continuity Through Selective Loss

**Project:** Mirror Cartographer  
**Status:** Evidence-grounded comparative research dossier; hypothesis-generating, not a systematic review  
**Created:** 2026-11-02  
**Research question:** How do living systems preserve organismal continuity through selective removal, isolation, recycling, replacement, and reintegration—and how do these capacities change with age or become dysregulated in disease?

> **Scope boundary:** This dossier compares plant and animal mechanisms at the level of biological organization. It does not claim that plant abscission, mammalian cell extrusion, autophagy, apoptosis, immune clearance, psychological grief, and muscular relaxation are one mechanism. It does not infer plant consciousness or subjective grief. It does not establish a universal theory of aging, cancer, or disease.

## 1. Why this question follows Expedition 001

Expedition 001, *The Signal That Leaves the Wound*, asks how injury information is encoded, propagated, limited, and interpreted. This expedition asks what happens after a living system receives that information: how does it preserve the larger organism while changing or removing components?

The shared research problem is not simply "release." It is **continuity through regulated change**.

A living system must sometimes:
- retain and repair a component;
- recycle damaged intracellular material;
- remove a cell while preserving tissue integrity;
- isolate a damaged region;
- shed a structure;
- replace a lost component;
- reorganize the remaining system.

The word *release* is a useful umbrella question, but not a single biological pathway. Each process must be named at its actual mechanistic level.

## 2. Central synthesis

**Provisional organizing proposition:** Biological continuity depends in part on the ability to coordinate preservation, selective removal, containment, replacement, and reintegration across nested levels of organization.

This proposition is a cross-domain synthesis. The component mechanisms are established in particular systems; the claim that they form a general comparative framework remains to be tested.

The relevant levels are:

1. **Intracellular:** selective degradation and recycling of components (for example, autophagy).
2. **Cellular:** cell-cycle arrest, programmed cell death, extrusion, and immune-mediated clearance.
3. **Tissue:** barrier maintenance, local containment, remodeling, and regeneration.
4. **Organismal:** organ shedding, compensatory growth, behavioral and physiological adaptation.
5. **Experiential:** conscious responses to bodily or social loss, where a nervous system capable of such experience is present. Physiological response alone does not establish subjective experience.

The organism is not preserved by keeping every component unchanged. But selective loss is not automatically beneficial: the timing, target, containment, replacement capacity, and consequences determine whether it preserves or harms the whole.

## 3. Evidence map

### A. Plants: regulated abscission is a specialized separation process

**Li & Su (2024), “Abscission in plants: from mechanism to applications,” Advanced Biotechnology.**  
https://doi.org/10.1007/s44307-024-00033-9  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11740850/

- **Source type:** Review.
- **Reported scope:** Plant organ separation in response to developmental and environmental signals, including stress and aging.
- **Mechanistic summary:** Abscission involves differentiation of a separation zone, activation, tissue detachment, and formation of a protective layer. Nutrient remobilization can occur before separation.
- **Boundary:** Abscission is not simply passive shedding. It is regulated, species- and organ-dependent, and can be adaptive or agriculturally costly when excessive.
- **Why it matters:** Plants provide a well-characterized model of separating a structure while protecting the remaining organism.

**Pautot, Crick & Hepworth (2025), “Abscission zones: cellular interfaces for the programmed separation of organs,” Annals of Botany.**  
https://doi.org/10.1093/aob/mcaf034  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40045663/

- **Source type:** Review.
- **Reported scope:** Abscission-zone development and operation in Arabidopsis and tomato.
- **Mechanistic summary:** Abscission zones are specialized interfaces that pass through initiation, competence, separation, and sealing.
- **Boundary:** The review focuses on selected model plants; it does not establish one invariant mechanism across all plant lineages.
- **Why it matters:** Separation is spatially prepared at a boundary. The interface is part of the mechanism, not an afterthought.

### B. Plants: intracellular recycling and quality control

**Gross et al. (2025), “Autophagy in Plant Health and Disease,” Annual Review of Plant Biology 76:197–227.**  
https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-arplant-060324-094912

- **Source type:** Review.
- **Reported scope:** Plant autophagy in health, disease, development, and stress.
- **Reported finding:** Autophagy selectively removes damaged or unwanted cellular components, including organelles, protein aggregates, and invading microbes, supporting cellular homeostasis.
- **Boundary:** Autophagy is not equivalent to whole-cell death, organ shedding, or tissue regeneration. Its effects depend on cargo, context, and regulation.
- **Why it matters:** A cell can preserve its identity by changing its contents. Selective internal turnover is distinct from destroying the cell.

### C. Plants: compartmentalization of damage in trees

**Morris et al. (2016), “The Parenchyma of Secondary Xylem and Its Critical Role in Tree Defense against Fungal Decay in Relation to the CODIT Model,” Frontiers in Plant Science 7:1665.**  
https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2016.01665  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5101214/

- **Source type:** Review.
- **Reported scope:** Living parenchyma, anatomical structure, and defense against fungal decay in secondary xylem.
- **Reported finding:** Pre-existing anatomical divisions and active responses of living parenchyma cooperate to form boundaries that restrict air or decay spread.
- **Boundary:** CODIT is a simplifying model. Actual defense depends on three-dimensional tissue connectivity and substantial variation among species and wood anatomies. Compartmentalization restricts spread; it does not restore damaged wood to its former state.
- **Why it matters:** A tree can preserve organismal continuity by limiting the spread of local damage rather than fully replacing the damaged structure.

### D. Mammals: tissues also remove cells while preserving the whole

**Eisenhoffer et al. (2012), “Crowding induces live cell extrusion to maintain homeostatic cell numbers in epithelia,” Nature 484:546–549.**  
https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10999  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22504183/

- **Source type:** Primary research.
- **Models:** Human, canine, and zebrafish epithelial cells; in vivo zebrafish observations and in vitro epithelial monolayers.
- **Reported finding:** Epithelial crowding can induce live-cell extrusion. In zebrafish, disrupting Piezo1 impaired extrusion and produced epithelial cell masses in the tested model.
- **Boundary:** This is evidence for particular epithelial systems, not a claim that all mammalian tissues use the same process or that cell extrusion alone prevents cancer.
- **Why it matters:** It directly refutes the oversimplification that plants can selectively release parts whereas animals cannot. Mammalian tissues also have regulated mechanisms for removing cells while maintaining a barrier.

**Gudipaty & Rosenblatt (2017), “Epithelial Cell Extrusion: Pathways and Pathologies,” Seminars in Cell & Developmental Biology.**  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5116298/

- **Source type:** Review.
- **Reported scope:** Apoptotic and live-cell extrusion, epithelial homeostasis, barrier integrity, and disease.
- **Reported finding:** Neighboring cells can coordinate actomyosin contraction and extrusion; crowding-related live extrusion can remove cells from an epithelial layer while maintaining barrier continuity.
- **Boundary:** The pathways differ by context, and pathogens or tumors can manipulate extrusion-related mechanisms.
- **Why it matters:** The remaining tissue must close the space left by a departing cell. Removal and continuity are coordinated.

### E. Aging: a specific clearance mechanism can decline, with functional consequences in mice

**Sereda et al. (2026), “Decline of chaperone-mediated autophagy in aging impairs macrophage clearance of senescent cells,” Nature Aging.**  
https://doi.org/10.1038/s43587-026-01240-w

- **Source type:** Primary research.
- **Reported finding:** Age-related decline in chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA) altered senescent-cell properties and impaired macrophage clearance. In mice, macrophage CMA disruption increased senescent-cell accumulation and delayed senescence resolution during wound healing. Pharmacological CMA activation reduced senescent-cell burden in aged mice and disease severity in a pulmonary-fibrosis mouse model.
- **Human evidence:** Human lung datasets and tissue samples showed age- and disease-associated CMA changes, but the authors explicitly describe the human lung data as correlative and requiring patient validation.
- **Important limitations:** Fibroblasts were the primary senescence model; cell-type specificity needs further work. The macrophage genetic model may affect other myeloid cells. The intervention results are preclinical and do not establish a human anti-aging treatment.
- **Why it matters:** This is a concrete, recent example of the proposed continuity problem: a specific intracellular maintenance pathway affects the capacity of immune cells to clear senescent cells, and impaired clearance is linked experimentally to delayed resolution in mice.

**Kirkland & Tchkonia (2023), “Targeting Efferocytosis in Inflammaging.”**  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37585658/

- **Source type:** Review.
- **Reported finding:** Efferocytosis—the uptake and clearance of apoptotic cells by phagocytes—supports tissue homeostasis, inflammation resolution, and repair. Impaired clearance can allow dying-cell material to accumulate and promote sterile inflammation.
- **Boundary:** This review synthesizes mechanisms and therapeutic opportunities; it does not establish that all age-related inflammation is caused by impaired efferocytosis.
- **Why it matters:** It links removal, immune regulation, and tissue repair while preserving the distinction between a mechanism and a clinical intervention.

### F. Cancer: cell-level persistence can conflict with organism-level continuity

**Aktipis et al. (2015), “Cancer across the tree of life: cooperation and cheating in multicellularity,” Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B.**  
https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2014.0220  
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4581024/

- **Source type:** Evolutionary review.
- **Framework:** Multicellular cooperation depends on constraints on proliferation, controlled cell death, resource allocation, division of labor, and maintenance of the extracellular environment.
- **Interpretation:** Cancer-like processes can be understood as breakdowns in one or more forms of cellular cooperation.
- **Boundary:** This is an evolutionary framework, not a complete molecular explanation of every cancer. Tumors are heterogeneous and disease-specific.
- **Why it matters:** It provides a rigorous way to examine conflict between cell-level selection and organism-level integrity.

**Maley et al. (2025), “Multicellular cooperation and the hallmarks of cancer: A new foundation,” Evolution, Medicine, and Public Health 13:374–380.**  
https://doi.org/10.1093/emph/eoaf026

- **Source type:** Conceptual/evolutionary review.
- **Contribution:** Revisits the foundations of multicellular cooperation and proposes proximity maintenance as an additional foundation relevant to cancer and invasion.
- **Boundary:** The proposed framework organizes evolutionary thinking; it is not a single experimentally established pathway or universal cancer mechanism.
- **Why it matters:** It reinforces that multicellular identity depends on cooperation, communication, resource distribution, and physical organization—not merely the survival of individual cells.

## 4. What is genuinely shared—and what is not

| Comparison | Relationship type | What is shared | What differs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plant autophagy / animal autophagy | Conserved eukaryotic process | Intracellular degradation and recycling | Cargo, regulation, tissue context |
| Plant abscission / mammalian epithelial extrusion | Structural analogy | Controlled separation at a tissue interface | Distinct developmental and molecular machinery |
| Tree compartmentalization / mammalian tissue containment | Functional analogy | Restriction of local damage spread | Anatomy, cell types, immune architecture |
| Plant autophagy / mammalian efferocytosis | Complementary, not equivalent | Clearance contributes to homeostasis | Intracellular self-recycling versus phagocytic removal of other cells |
| Epithelial extrusion / cancer suppression | Mechanistic relationship in studied systems | Removal can limit excess or abnormal cells | Extrusion can also be hijacked or dysregulated |
| Senescent-cell persistence / aging | Supported relationship in specific models | Reduced clearance can contribute to persistent senescent burden | Senescence can be beneficial transiently; not all aging is clearance failure |
| Cancer / organismal self-maintenance | Evolutionary framework | Cellular cooperation supports multicellular integrity | Cancer involves diverse genetic, epigenetic, ecological, and evolutionary processes |

**Key correction:** "Plants can release; animals cannot" is false. Both plants and animals possess multiple forms of selective removal, containment, and replacement. Their architectures and regenerative capacities differ. The useful comparison is not presence versus absence, but mechanism, scale, timing, modularity, and functional consequence.

## 5. Claim register and current proof status

### C1 — Plants have regulated organ abscission and protective separation interfaces
- **Type:** Source-reported finding.
- **Status:** Supported.
- **Evidence:** Li & Su (2024); Pautot et al. (2025).
- **Confidence:** High for the studied plant systems.
- **Not established:** One universal abscission mechanism across all plants; subjective experience of loss.
- **Next test:** Compare abscission-zone architecture and regulation across selected species and organs.

### C2 — Plant cells use autophagy to selectively remove and recycle damaged or unwanted components
- **Type:** Source-reported finding.
- **Status:** Supported.
- **Evidence:** Gross et al. (2025).
- **Confidence:** High as a broad plant-cell mechanism.
- **Not established:** That autophagy alone explains whole-organism longevity or regenerative superiority.
- **Next test:** Compare autophagic flux and functional outcomes across age, tissue, and stress conditions.

### C3 — Trees can compartmentalize decay and restrict damage spread
- **Type:** Source-reported finding.
- **Status:** Supported with anatomical and species-specific qualification.
- **Evidence:** Morris et al. (2016).
- **Confidence:** High for the reviewed mechanisms; generalization depends on species and wood anatomy.
- **Not established:** Complete restoration of damaged wood or immunity to decay.
- **Next test:** Link boundary formation to measured pathogen spread and long-term structural function.

### C4 — Mammalian epithelia can selectively extrude cells while maintaining tissue barriers
- **Type:** Source-reported finding.
- **Status:** Supported in tested epithelial models.
- **Evidence:** Eisenhoffer et al. (2012); Gudipaty & Rosenblatt (2017).
- **Confidence:** High for the studied models.
- **Not established:** Universal operation across mammalian tissues or a standalone cancer-prevention mechanism.
- **Next test:** Measure extrusion, barrier integrity, and tissue outcome together under controlled perturbations.

### C5 — Age-related impairment of a specific cellular quality-control pathway can impair immune clearance and delay repair
- **Type:** Source-reported finding.
- **Status:** Supported in preclinical models; human relevance preliminary.
- **Evidence:** Sereda et al. (2026).
- **Confidence:** High for the reported experimental mouse and cell models; limited for human causal inference.
- **Not established:** A general human aging mechanism, a human treatment, or reversal of organismal aging.
- **Next test:** Independent replication, additional cell types and tissues, and prospective human validation with direct functional measures.

### C6 — Cancer can be framed as a breakdown of multicellular cooperation
- **Type:** Evolutionary synthesis.
- **Status:** Supported as a useful framework, not a complete mechanism.
- **Evidence:** Aktipis et al. (2015); Maley et al. (2025).
- **Confidence:** Moderate-high as an organizing evolutionary account.
- **Not established:** That all cancers share one cause or that cancer is simply a failure to release.
- **Next test:** Map specific tumor phenotypes to specific cooperation failures and test causal predictions in defined models.

### C7 — Aging generally reflects a declining ability to release, isolate, and reintegrate components
- **Type:** Cross-domain hypothesis.
- **Status:** Plausible but unproven as a general proposition.
- **Evidence:** Convergent but mechanism-specific findings on autophagy, senescence, efferocytosis, stem-cell function, and intercellular communication.
- **Confidence:** Low-to-moderate as a unified theory; higher for individual mechanisms.
- **Not established:** A single latent "release capacity" that declines uniformly across organisms, tissues, or people.
- **Next test:** Operationalize distinct capacities and test whether their longitudinal changes predict functional aging beyond established covariates.

### C8 — Successful biological continuity requires coordination between component-level change and whole-system function
- **Type:** Cross-domain synthesis.
- **Status:** Strong organizing proposition; empirical scope remains to be tested.
- **Evidence:** Plant abscission and compartmentalization; mammalian epithelial extrusion; intracellular autophagy; efferocytosis; multicellular cooperation literature.
- **Confidence:** High as a descriptive systems principle, not yet a single quantitative law.
- **Next test:** Compare whether measurable coordination among removal, containment, replacement, and function predicts outcomes within defined biological models.

## 6. Adversarial review: attacks on the attractive idea

### Attack 1 — Category collapse
**Challenge:** "Release" combines relaxation, autophagy, apoptosis, efferocytosis, extrusion, abscission, compartmentalization, and grief. Are these actually one mechanism?

**Disposition:** Rejected as a single-mechanism claim. Retain "selective change/continuity" as an umbrella framework, with typed mechanisms and typed cross-domain relationships.

### Attack 2 — Anthropomorphism
**Challenge:** Does plant electrical or chemical signaling prove that trees feel grief when leaves fall?

**Disposition:** No. Physiological signaling and subjective experience are distinct evidentiary questions. Plant consciousness/grief is not established by these sources and is excluded from factual conclusions.

### Attack 3 — Teleology
**Challenge:** Does the organism "expect" to heal or intentionally decide what to remove?

**Disposition:** Avoid purposive language unless describing an experimentally established function. Use developmental regulation, selection, signaling, and functional consequence instead.

### Attack 4 — False plant/animal binary
**Challenge:** Does comparison imply that plants can selectively shed while animals cannot?

**Disposition:** Falsified by mammalian epithelial extrusion and mammalian autophagy/efferocytosis. Reframe around differences in architecture, modularity, regenerative capacity, and tissue-specific mechanisms.

### Attack 5 — Longevity fallacy
**Challenge:** Does a long-lived tree prove that plants avoid aging or have superior release capacity?

**Disposition:** No. Tree longevity, modular growth, clonal persistence, and individual cell longevity are different quantities. A tree's continued existence does not mean every part remains young or that it is immortal.

### Attack 6 — Cancer as unfinished healing
**Challenge:** Is cancer simply a wound-healing process that failed to finish?

**Disposition:** Too broad and potentially misleading. Some tumors exploit wound-repair and inflammatory pathways, but cancer involves somatic evolution and loss of multicellular cooperation through diverse mechanisms. Keep the wound analogy as a limited comparison, not an explanation of cancer as a whole.

### Attack 7 — Aging as universal failure to release
**Challenge:** Does every organism or tissue lose the same release capacity with age?

**Disposition:** Not established. Aging is heterogeneous; some functions decline, others are maintained or compensated. The 2026 CMA study is a specific mechanism and model, not a universal result.

### Attack 8 — Biomarker substitution
**Challenge:** Could reduced inflammation, a normalized hormone, or a visible behavioral change be mistaken for restored function?

**Disposition:** Yes. Require direct functional outcomes and longitudinal context. A biomarker change alone cannot establish successful restoration.

### Attack 9 — Cross-species overgeneralization
**Challenge:** Can a mechanism in Arabidopsis, zebrafish, cultured epithelia, or aged mice be directly applied to humans?

**Disposition:** No. First validate within each model. Cross-species transfer requires separate evidence for conserved mechanism, tissue context, safety, and functional outcome.

### Attack 10 — Survivorship and sampling
**Challenge:** Are the organisms or tissues studied representative of all ages, sexes, species, or disease states?

**Disposition:** Not automatically. Record species, strain/cultivar, sex, age, tissue, environment, sample size, and selection method. Treat small human tissue samples as preliminary.

## 7. Hypotheses generated by the synthesis

### H1 — Continuity depends on coordinated turnover, not maximal preservation
**Proposition:** Within a defined tissue, functional continuity is better predicted by the coordination of removal, barrier maintenance, replacement, and reintegration than by the amount of cell survival alone.

**Competing explanation:** Tissue function is determined primarily by injury burden, vascular supply, or stem-cell availability; removal dynamics add little predictive value.

**Disconfirmation:** In a controlled model, adding prespecified turnover and boundary metrics does not improve prediction of functional recovery beyond injury severity and tissue context.

### H2 — Clearance capacity is one measurable contributor to age-related recovery
**Proposition:** In selected mammalian tissues, impaired intracellular quality control and phagocytic clearance contribute to persistence of senescent or dying cells and delayed restoration.

**Competing explanation:** Clearance impairment is downstream of tissue damage and does not materially drive functional decline.

**Disconfirmation:** Restoring the relevant clearance pathway changes clearance biomarkers but does not improve tissue function or recovery in independent models.

### H3 — Modular architecture changes the cost of local loss
**Proposition:** Organisms and tissues with modular architecture can preserve whole-system function after local component loss more readily than highly integrated structures, conditional on resource supply and replacement capacity.

**Competing explanation:** Apparent modularity effects are explained by differences in vascularization, developmental plasticity, or environmental exposure.

**Disconfirmation:** Across appropriately matched models, modularity does not predict functional continuity after standardized local loss once those factors are controlled.

### H4 — Boundary quality mediates whether removal preserves or damages the whole
**Proposition:** Selective removal is beneficial when surrounding boundaries remain functional and the remaining tissue can close, seal, or reorganize; poorly contained removal can compromise the larger system.

**Competing explanation:** The decisive factor is total damage burden rather than boundary function.

**Disconfirmation:** Manipulating boundary integrity while holding injury burden comparable does not change spread, barrier function, or long-term outcome.

### H5 — Cancer risk can increase when cell-level persistence escapes tissue-level constraints
**Proposition:** In specified epithelial contexts, impaired extrusion or cell competition can permit abnormal cell accumulation and contribute to tumor-promoting conditions.

**Competing explanation:** Genetic driver alterations dominate, with extrusion failure occurring as a secondary consequence.

**Disconfirmation:** Restoring extrusion or cell competition in a defined model fails to alter abnormal-cell persistence or tumor-related outcomes after relevant genetic drivers are controlled.

## 8. Experimental program

These are research designs for qualified laboratories, not instructions for unsupervised biological experimentation.

### Study A — Plant separation and continuity
- **Models:** Select two plant species with documented abscission and contrasting growth architectures.
- **Measurements:** Abscission-zone differentiation, nutrient remobilization, separation timing, wound sealing, subsequent growth, and resource allocation.
- **Controls:** Developmental versus stress-induced shedding; intact versus experimentally removed structures; matched environmental conditions.
- **Primary outcome:** Continued whole-plant function and subsequent growth, not merely successful detachment.
- **Question:** Which features of separation predict successful continuation rather than costly or excessive loss?

### Study B — Tree compartmentalization
- **Models:** Selected woody species with contrasting xylem anatomy.
- **Measurements:** Anatomical boundary formation, pathogen spread, living parenchyma response, decay progression, and structural function over time.
- **Primary outcome:** Extent of containment and preservation of functional tissue.
- **Question:** Which anatomical and active barriers best predict containment, and how much does species architecture matter?

### Study C — Mammalian epithelial extrusion
- **Models:** Established epithelial culture and an appropriate validated in vivo model.
- **Measurements:** Extrusion rate, cell viability at extrusion, Piezo1/S1P pathway status, barrier permeability, cell density, and subsequent tissue function.
- **Controls:** Sham conditions, matched crowding, pathway perturbations, viability and toxicity controls.
- **Primary outcome:** Whether selective removal preserves barrier function and controls abnormal cell accumulation.
- **Question:** When does extrusion protect tissue, and when can the process be disrupted or hijacked?

### Study D — Aging and immune clearance
- **Models:** Young and aged mammalian systems, beginning with independent replication of the CMA/efferocytosis findings.
- **Measurements:** CMA activity, macrophage uptake and degradation of senescent/apoptotic cells, senescent-cell burden, inflammatory signaling, wound resolution, and organ-specific function.
- **Controls:** Cell-type-specific perturbations, vehicle controls, sex-stratified analysis, multiple tissues, and direct functional endpoints.
- **Primary outcome:** Restored clearance accompanied by improved tissue function, not a biomarker shift alone.
- **Question:** Which clearance deficits are causal, reversible, tissue-specific, and functionally important?

### Study E — Cross-model comparison
- **Design:** Compare abstract functional variables, not presumed identical molecules: selective removal rate, containment integrity, replacement capacity, reintegration latency, and whole-system function.
- **Analysis:** Model each species and tissue separately first. Only then test whether shared predictors generalize across models.
- **Primary outcome:** Out-of-sample prediction of functional continuity.
- **Question:** Are there transferable organizational principles, or are the apparent similarities only analogies?

## 9. Measurement architecture: do not compress distinct mechanisms into one score too early

Candidate variables:

| Construct | Possible measurement | Main caution |
|---|---|---|
| Selective intracellular recycling | Autophagic flux and cargo turnover | Static marker abundance is not flux |
| Cell removal | Apoptosis, extrusion, efferocytosis rates | Different processes; do not pool without justification |
| Containment | Barrier integrity, pathogen/decay spread, boundary anatomy | Different tissues have different boundary mechanisms |
| Replacement | Progenitor activity, stem-cell function, tissue growth | More proliferation is not necessarily better |
| Reintegration | Restored tissue architecture and physiological function | Requires tissue-specific endpoints |
| Recovery dynamics | Time to functional baseline or stable new state | Baseline may shift with age or adaptation |
| Whole-system continuity | Organism-level performance and survival | Must avoid substituting lifespan for healthspan |

Potential composite metrics should be developed only after construct validity is demonstrated. Do not invent a universal "release score" from incomparable measurements.

**Current proof verdict:** The strongest defensible result is that plants and animals both possess multiple regulated mechanisms for intracellular recycling, cell or organ removal, damage containment, and tissue continuity. A specific 2026 mammalian study strengthens the case that age-related decline in a cellular quality-control pathway can impair immune clearance and delay repair in preclinical models. The broader proposition—that aging is partly a loss of coordinated capacity for selective release and reintegration—is plausible, testable, and not yet proven.

## 11. What this expedition does not establish

- A universal biological law of release.
- A single cause of aging or death.
- That plants are immortal or do not age.
- That plants experience grief or human-like emotion.
- That fear is the primary cause of chronic disease.
- That cancer is simply unfinished healing.
- That all disease is reversible.
- That plant mechanisms can be directly transferred to human treatment.
- That increasing cell removal, autophagy, apoptosis, or immune clearance is always beneficial.
- A validated diagnostic score or therapeutic target.
- Any experimental result generated by Mirror Cartographer.

## 12. Next proof gates

1. **Literature completeness:** systematic searches across plant abscission, tree compartmentalization, autophagy, epithelial extrusion, efferocytosis, senescence, cell competition, cancer, and comparative aging; record databases, date, query, inclusion/exclusion criteria.
2. **Primary-study verification:** extract methods, sample sizes, species/strains, sex, tissue, interventions, endpoints, statistical methods, corrections, and replication status.
3. **Counterevidence:** actively search for null results, contexts where shedding or clearance is harmful, and examples where regeneration fails despite successful removal.
4. **Construct validation:** determine whether selective turnover, containment, replacement, and reintegration can be measured separately and reliably.
5. **Causal tests:** prioritize controlled perturbations with functional endpoints and independent replication.
6. **Cross-species test:** only after within-species models work; use typed relationships and do not assume homologous mechanisms.
7. **External review:** obtain plant biology, cell biology, immunology, geroscience, cancer evolution, and comparative physiology review before any stronger public claim.
