# Cancer Research Frontier — A Program for Prevention, Selective Elimination, and Durable Control

**Status:** research program / hypothesis map  
**Owner:** Mirror Cartographer public research collection  
**Evidence rule:** distinguish established findings, active research, proposed hypotheses, and speculation. A compelling mechanism is not a treatment until it survives validation.

## Mission

Reduce cancer incidence, detect dangerous transitions earlier, selectively eliminate malignant cells, and prevent relapse while preserving healthy tissue and quality of life.

There is no single cancer. Tumours differ by tissue, cell lineage, genome, epigenetic state, immune environment, metabolism, microbiome, treatment history, and evolutionary trajectory. Therefore, do not search for one magic molecule. Search for shared mechanisms, context-specific dependencies, and combinations that remain effective as tumours evolve.

This is a research map, not medical advice, a treatment protocol, or a claim that a cure has been found. No experimental intervention should be attempted in a person or animal outside appropriate clinical and veterinary oversight.

## The central organizing model

Treat cancer as a multiscale evolving system:

1. **Origin:** inherited susceptibility, acquired mutations, epigenetic change, infection, exposures, tissue injury, ageing, and stochastic events.
2. **Selection:** cell populations compete and adapt; clones with survival or growth advantages expand.
3. **Niche:** tumour cells interact with immune cells, fibroblasts, vasculature, extracellular matrix, nerves, microbes, and distant organs.
4. **Escape:** cells evade immune recognition, resist death, change identity, alter metabolism, invade, and disseminate.
5. **Treatment pressure:** therapy removes sensitive populations and can select resistant states.
6. **Persistence:** residual cells may remain dormant, slow-cycling, spatially protected, or phenotypically altered.
7. **Recurrence / metastasis:** surviving populations re-expand or colonize new tissue.

A useful intervention must specify which stage it changes, in which tumour context, by what mechanism, and how that change will be measured.

## Research lanes

### A. Prevention and interception

Questions:
- Which causal exposures and infections are preventable?
- Which precancerous states are reversible, and which are likely to progress?
- Can high-risk tissue states be identified before invasive disease?
- Can prevention be targeted to the relevant tissue and risk group without unacceptable harm?

Methods:
- prospective cohorts and well-designed epidemiology;
- molecular characterization of precancerous tissue;
- causal inference with explicit confounder analysis;
- validated screening and risk-stratification studies;
- prevention-vaccine and infection-elimination research where evidence supports it.

Do not confuse association with causation or screening detection with prevention.

### B. Tumour-specific dependencies and synthetic lethality

Question:
What does a particular cancer cell require because of its exact genetic, epigenetic, metabolic, or lineage state, while the matched healthy cell does not?

Priorities:
- DNA damage response and repair dependencies;
- loss of redundant pathways;
- replication stress;
- lineage-specific transcriptional dependencies;
- metabolic bottlenecks;
- context-dependent signalling dependencies.

Method:
1. Define tumour genotype, lineage, state, and treatment history.
2. Identify candidate dependencies in perturbation datasets.
3. Compare malignant models with relevant non-malignant controls.
4. Replicate across independent models and patient-derived samples.
5. Test whether the dependency persists across subclones and adaptive states.
6. Establish a therapeutic window, not merely a tumour-killing signal.
7. Identify biomarkers that predict response and resistance.

A target is not promising merely because disabling it kills a cancer cell line. Selectivity, reproducibility, delivery, toxicity, and evolutionary escape matter.

### C. Immune recognition and immune escape

Questions:
- Which tumour subclones are visible to immune surveillance?
- Which escape mechanisms prevent antigen presentation or T-cell function?
- Can immune recognition be broadened without provoking unacceptable autoimmunity?
- How do local tissue conditions suppress immune activity?

Map:
- antigen expression and presentation;
- HLA / MHC status;
- T-cell and NK-cell states;
- immune checkpoint pathways;
- suppressive myeloid populations;
- tumour-associated stromal barriers;
- spatial distribution of immune cells;
- clonal heterogeneity and immunoediting.

A tumour is not immunologically uniform. A response in one region or clone does not establish control of the whole tumour.

### D. Cell-state plasticity and epigenetic adaptation

Questions:
- Can malignant cells change identity to evade a targeted treatment?
- Which cell states are reversible?
- Which state transitions precede resistance, invasion, or relapse?
- Can a tumour be pushed into a state that is more vulnerable to an independently validated intervention?

Methods:
- single-cell RNA and chromatin profiling;
- lineage tracing in appropriate models;
- time-course sampling before, during, and after treatment;
- perturbation studies;
- spatial profiling to retain tissue context.

Do not assume that a marker defines a stable cell identity. Measure transitions over time.

### E. Tumour metabolism and systemic context

Questions:
- Which metabolic dependencies are tumour-intrinsic?
- Which are imposed by oxygen, nutrients, acidity, or neighbouring cells?
- How does metabolism alter immune function?
- Which systemic host factors change tumour behaviour or treatment tolerance?

Methods:
- metabolomics and isotope-tracing in qualified laboratories;
- matched tumour / normal comparisons;
- spatial metabolite analysis;
- co-culture and organoid models;
- careful integration with host physiology.

Avoid simplistic claims that cancer is caused by sugar, acidity, or one metabolic pathway. Tumours use diverse and adaptable metabolic programs.

### F. Microenvironment, invasion, and metastasis

Questions:
- Which interactions enable local invasion?
- What makes a distant organ permissive to metastatic colonization?
- Which disseminated cells persist without growing?
- Can early dissemination or niche formation be interrupted safely?

Map:
- extracellular matrix and mechanics;
- fibroblast and endothelial states;
- immune suppression;
- vascular entry and exit;
- organ-specific niches;
- circulating tumour cells and disseminated tumour cells;
- dormancy and reactivation.

Metastasis is a sequence of bottlenecks, not one event. Measure each step separately.

### G. Minimal residual disease, dormancy, and recurrence

This is a high-priority lane because apparent tumour removal does not necessarily establish eradication.

Questions:
- What residual populations remain after treatment?
- Which assays detect them reliably?
- Which residual states are viable, dormant, or destined to regrow?
- What predicts reactivation?
- Can recurrence be prevented without chronic unacceptable toxicity?

Methods:
- longitudinal circulating tumour DNA where clinically validated;
- tissue-based residual-disease analysis;
- serial imaging and pathology;
- single-cell and spatial analysis in research settings;
- recurrence-focused model systems;
- long follow-up with clearly defined endpoints.

Do not treat a negative test as proof that no malignant cell remains. Report assay sensitivity, sampling limits, and false-negative risk.

### H. Functional precision medicine

Genomic sequencing alone does not always reveal which therapy will work. A complementary approach tests living patient-derived material against candidate treatments in a controlled research setting.

Research questions:
- Which ex vivo assays predict patient response?
- How well do organoids, explants, and other models preserve tumour heterogeneity?
- Which assay results reproduce across laboratories?
- How quickly can results be returned without compromising reliability?
- What clinical outcomes validate the assay?

This requires appropriate tissue access, consent, biosafety, laboratory quality systems, and prospective clinical validation. It is not a do-it-yourself procedure.

## Cross-cutting discovery engine

Every candidate idea must be represented as:

- **Observation:** what was actually measured?
- **Source:** where did the observation originate?
- **Population / model:** which cancer, tissue, genotype, and experimental system?
- **Claim:** what is being asserted?
- **Mechanism:** how could the effect occur?
- **Alternative explanations:** what else could produce the observation?
- **Evidence level:** established, replicated preclinical, early clinical, validated clinical, or speculative.
- **Counterevidence:** what findings disagree?
- **Selectivity:** what happens to matched healthy tissue?
- **Durability:** does the effect survive tumour adaptation?
- **Endpoint:** what measurable outcome would count as success?
- **Next experiment:** what is the smallest decisive test?

## Validation ladder

1. **Computational signal:** reproducible analysis of public data; no causal claim.
2. **Independent dataset replication:** same direction in a separate cohort or dataset.
3. **Mechanistic perturbation:** targeted perturbation supports the proposed causal mechanism.
4. **Relevant model replication:** effect reproduced in multiple appropriate models, including non-malignant controls.
5. **Patient-derived model:** evidence in a model retaining relevant patient tumour features.
6. **Preclinical safety and efficacy:** appropriate pharmacology, delivery, toxicity, and efficacy studies under qualified oversight.
7. **Clinical testing:** ethically reviewed, registered trials with predefined endpoints and monitoring.
8. **Independent replication and clinical adoption:** reproducible benefit, meaningful outcomes, and acceptable harms.

A result cannot skip levels because it is exciting.

## Work possible without owning a laboratory

The project can contribute meaningfully before obtaining wet-lab access:

- build a traceable literature map;
- catalogue public datasets and their limitations;
- reproduce published computational analyses;
- compare findings across independent cohorts;
- identify contradictions and missing controls;
- rank hypotheses by novelty, evidence, tractability, and potential impact;
- write preregistered analysis plans;
- create collaboration briefs for qualified laboratories;
- prepare clear, testable proposals for institutional researchers.

Potential public resources to evaluate include:
- The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA): tumour molecular and clinical datasets.
- NCI Genomic Data Commons: cancer genomic data and metadata.
- DepMap: cancer-cell dependency and molecular data.
- cBioPortal: exploration of cancer genomics studies.
- Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO): deposited functional genomics datasets.
- ClinicalTrials.gov: registered clinical studies.
- Human Tumor Atlas Network: spatial and single-cell tumour atlas resources.
- CPTAC: proteomic characterization of cancer.

Before analysis, inspect each dataset's access terms, consent constraints, sample provenance, clinical annotations, batch effects, missingness, and intended use. Controlled-access data must not be treated as public.

## First concrete work packages

### WP-01 — Build the evidence atlas
Create a structured index of cancer mechanisms, datasets, interventions, model systems, and evidence levels. Every record gets a source and last-checked date.

### WP-02 — Find recurring selective vulnerabilities
Compare synthetic-lethal and dependency findings across cancer types. Prioritize findings with matched normal-cell controls and independent replication.

### WP-03 — Map immune escape by tumour context
Separate tumour-intrinsic, immune-cell, stromal, and spatial mechanisms. Track heterogeneity rather than assigning one label to an entire tumour.

### WP-04 — Investigate residual disease and relapse
Map how residual populations are measured, missed, maintained, and reactivated. Distinguish detection limits from biological absence.

### WP-05 — Audit functional precision models
Compare organoid, explant, cell-line, and other patient-derived model performance against clinical response. Identify where model predictions fail.

### WP-06 — Prevention and precancer atlas
Map established causal exposures, infection-linked cancers, precursor lesions, and validated prevention approaches. Keep risk reduction separate from treatment.

## Priority scoring

Score each hypothesis from 0–5 on:
- strength and independence of evidence;
- biological plausibility;
- selectivity for malignant versus healthy tissue;
- relevance across more than one model;
- durability under evolutionary pressure;
- feasibility of a decisive test;
- potential clinical impact.

Also score risk of bias, missing controls, and uncertainty separately. Do not let a high novelty score compensate for weak evidence or poor safety.

## Stop conditions

Pause or reject a hypothesis when:
- the key result cannot be reproduced;
- the effect also damages relevant healthy cells;
- the proposed mechanism is contradicted by the data;
- model systems do not represent the intended disease context;
- the endpoint is a surrogate that does not support the claimed benefit;
- the intervention requires unsafe or unethical experimentation;
- the claim outruns the evidence.

## First deliverable

A source-linked, versioned research atlas that answers:
1. What is already known?
2. What remains disputed or unmeasured?
3. Which candidate mechanisms have the strongest selective evidence?
4. What decisive experiment would distinguish each hypothesis?
5. Which laboratory, dataset, or clinical research group could validate it?

**Success is not a dramatic theory. Success is a chain of evidence that survives attempts to disprove it.**
